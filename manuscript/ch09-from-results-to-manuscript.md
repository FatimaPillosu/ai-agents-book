## 9.1 The problem this chapter addresses

The final stretch of a scientific project — turning verified results into a manuscript, a set of figures, a data statement and a response to reviewers — consumes a share of a researcher's time out of all proportion to the intellectual novelty it contains. Much of that time goes not on argument but on transcription and reconciliation: reading numbers off a results table into the running text, regenerating a figure because an upstream file changed, reformatting citations to a journal's house style, and assembling a point-by-point reply to a review that arrived four months after the analysis was last touched. The work is exacting, because a single mismatch between a number in the abstract and the same number in a table is the kind of error reviewers and readers notice, and it is also the work most vulnerable to the ordinary failures of tired attention. The result across the environmental sciences, though seldom measured directly, is that the output side of scholarship absorbs a substantial fraction of the effort of a project while adding little that is scientific to it **[AUTHOR: if you have a defensible personal estimate — the share of a recent paper's total effort spent on manuscript assembly, figures and revision rather than analysis — it would anchor this claim; otherwise leave it qualitative]**.

Against this background, systems that can read a pipeline's artefacts, assemble a draft, regenerate a figure from the file that produced its underlying numbers, and draft a structured reply to a review deserve careful rather than reflexive attention, precisely because the output side is where their characteristic failure — fluent, plausible and wrong — is most dangerous. A manuscript is the point at which a project makes its claims to the wider community, and an agent that smooths prose can as easily smooth over an unsupported claim, invent a citation that fits the sentence, or state a result more strongly than the evidence carries. The position taken in this chapter is therefore narrower than the tooling would permit: agents are drafting and assembly instruments operating under continuous author control, never authors and never the interpretive authority over what the results mean. The chapter sets out how to generate figures and tables from pipeline artefacts rather than by hand, how to run a reviewer-response workflow in which a human holds sole interpretive authority, how the provenance records of Chapter 12 feed a disclosure statement, and how to keep the whole process on the reliable side of the capability boundary drawn in Chapter 1.

## 9.2 The conventional workflow and where it leaks

The conventional route from results to submitted manuscript is a sequence of manual transcriptions between tools that do not talk to one another, and every transcription is a point at which the manuscript can silently diverge from the analysis behind it. A typical path runs from analysis scripts producing numbers and arrays, to a plotting step producing figure files, to a word processor into which figures are pasted and numbers are typed, to a reference manager, and finally to a journal's submission system with its own formatting requirements; the artefacts at each stage are copies, not links, and a copy does not update when its source changes. The characteristic failures of this arrangement are well known to anyone who has assembled a paper under deadline: a figure in the submitted version was generated from an earlier data file than the table beside it; a value quoted in the abstract was updated in the table but not in the text; a significance figure survived from a draft in which the sample was smaller. These are not failures of competence but of an architecture that relies on human vigilance to keep half a dozen loosely coupled representations of the same numbers in agreement, and vigilance is exactly the resource a project has least of in its closing weeks (high confidence).

The reviewer-response stage concentrates the same weakness under worse conditions. A review typically arrives months after the analysis was last active, so the researcher must first reconstruct the state of a codebase and a dataset they have partly forgotten, then decide for each comment whether it calls for a new analysis, a clarification, or a reasoned disagreement, and finally assemble a reply that quotes the reviewer, states the response, and points to the specific changes made to the manuscript. Much of the surrounding labour is clerical — collating comments into a table, cross-referencing each to line numbers, re-running an analysis with one parameter changed, regenerating the affected figure — and it is performed at precisely the moment when the intellectual core of the task, deciding what each comment is really asking and whether it is right, most needs undistracted attention. The conventional workflow gives the clerical and the interpretive work to the same tired person in the same undifferentiated session, and the interpretive work suffers for it. This is the seam the agentic redesign aims at: not to automate the judgement, which must remain human, but to strip the clerical load away from it so that the judgement is made with more attention rather than less (moderate confidence).

## 9.3 The agentic redesign: assembly under author control

The redesign replaces manual transcription with regeneration from a single source of truth, and replaces undifferentiated effort with a division of labour that keeps interpretation human. Its organising principle is that every number and every figure in the manuscript should be traceable to a pipeline artefact and regenerable from it, so that a late change upstream propagates rather than requiring the author to remember every place the old value appeared. In practice this means figures are produced by a plotting step that reads the same result files the analysis wrote, tables are generated from those files rather than typed, and the numbers quoted in the running text are, wherever the format allows, drawn from the same artefacts through templating rather than transcribed by hand. An assembly agent sits over this arrangement not as an author but as an operator: it can regenerate the figure set when a file changes, assemble a table to the journal's column specification, check that a value quoted in the abstract matches the table it derives from, and flag mismatches for the author to resolve. The interpretive acts — what the result means, which claim it supports, how strongly to state it — remain entirely with the human, and the agent's outputs are proposals that the author accepts, edits or rejects at an explicit gate (high confidence in the pattern; the degree of automation that is safe varies with the manuscript).

The same principle governs prose drafting, with a sharper boundary because prose is where accountability is most easily laundered. An agent can usefully draft a methods section from a specification and a pipeline configuration, a data-availability statement from provenance records, or documentation of a workflow from its own logs, because these are descriptive tasks whose correctness the author can verify against artefacts that already exist. An agent can also usefully perform mechanical language work — enforcing a consistent term, tightening an over-long sentence, aligning citations to a required style — where the author reads every change. What an agent must not do is originate the claims of the paper, decide what the results establish, or produce text whose fluency obscures that a human has not personally verified the substance behind it; the discipline that prevents this is that the author remains the source of every claim and reads every sentence they put their name to, treating agent-drafted prose as a first draft to be interrogated rather than a finished product to be waved through. The provenance records described in Chapter 12 do double duty here: they are what the methods and data statements are generated from, and they are also the raw material of the disclosure statement that records how agents were used, so that a workflow which keeps good provenance for verification gets its disclosure almost for free (moderate confidence).

## 9.4 Journal and funder policy as a volatile landscape

Any treatment of AI use in manuscript preparation has to contend with the fact that the governing policies are numerous, inconsistent between publishers and funders, and revised faster than a printed book can track, so this chapter surveys them by class rather than by name and directs the reader to the repository for current specifics. Three broad classes cover most of what a researcher will encounter, and they are worth distinguishing because they impose different obligations. The first is **disclosure-required**: use of AI tools in preparing the manuscript is permitted but must be declared, typically in a designated statement, with varying granularity as to which tools and which tasks must be named. The second is **use-restricted**: certain tasks are permitted and others are not — a common pattern permits language editing and assistance with drafting while prohibiting the generation of figures, data or citations, and some restrict use in peer review itself, where confidential manuscripts must not be entered into external systems. The third is **authorship-barred**, which is close to universal and on which this book takes an unqualified position: an AI system cannot be listed as an author, because authorship entails accountability for the work and an instrument cannot be accountable (high confidence; this class is stable where the others are volatile).

The practical discipline that follows from a volatile landscape is to build the workflow so that it satisfies the strictest plausible reading of all three classes, and to generate the disclosure from records rather than from memory. Keeping provenance of which agent performed which task, on what inputs, under whose review (Chapter 12) means that a disclosure statement can be assembled to whatever granularity a given venue requires without reconstructing the history after the fact, and that a restriction encountered late — a journal that forbids agent-generated figures, say — can be checked against the record rather than guessed at. Because the specifics move, the print states the classes and the reasoning while the repository holds a current, dated summary of representative policies and a template disclosure statement mapped to each class **[AUTHOR: maintain the policy summary in the repository and date every entry; cite it here rather than naming any publisher's current policy in print]** **[verify: confirm the authorship-barred position against the current guidance of the major publishers and the main research-integrity bodies at time of release]**. The stance throughout is conservative: disclose more rather than less, treat the absence of a policy as a reason to apply best practice rather than a licence to omit disclosure, and never enter a confidential manuscript under review into a system whose data handling the reviewer's institution has not sanctioned (moderate-to-high confidence).

## 9.5 Worked example: a reviewer-response workflow

The reviewer-response stage illustrates the pattern at its sharpest, because it combines heavy clerical load with high interpretive stakes, and the design keeps the two strictly apart. The example concerns a revision of an operational hydrology manuscript after peer review **[AUTHOR: supply the paper and the review — even anonymised — so the specific comments, analyses and responses replace the scaffold below; the workflow shape is general, but the worth of the example is in the real interpretive calls you made]**. The workflow begins with the agent performing only clerical structuring: it parses the review into a table of individual comments, each with a stable identifier, the reviewer's exact wording quoted verbatim, and a proposed classification into one of a few types — a request for a new or changed analysis, a request for clarification, a factual correction, or a point of disagreement. Classification is a proposal, not a decision; the author reviews every row and corrects the type, because whether a comment is a genuine request for new work or a misunderstanding to be answered in prose is exactly the interpretive judgement the workflow exists to protect. At this stage nothing has been decided about the science, and the agent has touched no result and originated no claim; it has turned an unstructured document into an auditable list (high confidence in the shape of this step).

The interpretive core belongs to the author alone, and the workflow is arranged so that it happens with full attention rather than in the margins of clerical work. For each comment the author decides the substance of the response: whether the reviewer is right, what if any new analysis is warranted, and how the manuscript should change; these decisions are recorded by the author, in the author's words, as the authoritative content of the reply. Where a decision calls for re-running an analysis with one parameter changed and regenerating the affected figure and table, the agent executes that mechanical task against the existing pipeline — the same artefact-linked generation of §9.3 — and the author verifies the regenerated output against the result files before it is used. Only once the author has settled every response does the agent return to a clerical role, assembling the point-by-point reply document: for each comment it lays out the reviewer's quoted wording, the author's response as written, and a precise pointer to the change made in the manuscript, formatting the whole to the journal's expected structure. The author reads the assembled reply in full before submission, because the agent's fluency at producing a well-formed response document is precisely the property that could make an inadequately justified reply read as though it were adequate. The division of labour is the point: the agent does the structuring and assembly at both ends, the human does all of the judging in the middle, and the boundary between them is explicit rather than blurred (moderate-to-high confidence; the exact split depends on the review, and Chapter 13 catalogues what goes wrong when the boundary is allowed to drift).

## 9.6 Failure modes

The output side of scholarship has three characteristic failure modes, and each is dangerous precisely because the agent's fluency makes the failed output look finished. The first is **fabricated citations**, in which a system supplies a reference that is plausible in author, journal and year but does not exist, or attaches a real reference to a claim it does not in fact support. This failure is common enough, and consequential enough, that no citation produced or suggested by an agent enters a manuscript in this book's workflow without being resolved to a real, retrieved source and checked to support the specific sentence it is attached to — the same discipline set out for literature synthesis in Chapter 5, applied again at the drafting stage because the risk recurs wherever an agent generates prose that cites. The mechanism that catches it is external verification against a bibliographic source, never the model's own assurance that the reference is correct, because a model is a poor judge of the existence of what it has generated (high confidence; Chapter 13 catalogues real instances).

The second failure is **style laundering**: agent-smoothed prose that reads as authoritative and thereby obscures whether a human has actually verified the substance behind it, or whose uniform polish erases the seams where a claim is weak and would, in a human author's own prose, have shown its hesitation. This failure is subtler than fabrication because nothing in the output is factually wrong on its face; what is lost is the signal that ordinarily tells a reader — and the author's own re-reading — where the argument is thin. The discipline against it is that the author remains the source of every claim and reads every sentence critically for whether they can personally stand behind it, treating fluency as no evidence of correctness and resisting the temptation to accept a well-turned paragraph because it is well turned. The third failure is **over-claiming**, in which the drafted text states a result more strongly than the evidence carries — a correlation described as an effect, a result on one catchment generalised to a class, an uncertainty range quietly dropped in the abstract. Over-claiming is the failure most aligned with an agent's disposition to produce confident, readable prose, and the certainty-flag discipline of this book is the direct countermeasure: substantive claims carry an explicit statement of confidence, and a claim whose flag the author cannot justify is a claim to be weakened or removed. All three failures share a root and a remedy: the root is that the agent optimises for fluent, complete-looking output rather than for warranted output, and the remedy is that the human, not the agent, is accountable for every claim and verifies each against evidence external to the model (high confidence).

## 9.7 Verification checklist

The verification appropriate to the manuscript stage is a small set of checks applied before submission and again before any revision is returned, each keyed to a failure this chapter has described. The list below is the printable form; the repository holds it as a checklist artefact alongside the templates.

- **Every number in the text traces to an artefact.** Each value quoted in the abstract, results and captions matches the pipeline artefact it derives from; regenerate rather than transcribe, and re-check after any late upstream change (guards against divergence, §9.2).
- **Every figure and table is regenerated from source.** No figure or table in the submitted version was produced from a superseded data file; the generating step reads the current result files (guards against divergence, §9.3).
- **Every citation is real and supports its sentence.** Each reference is resolved to a retrieved source and checked to support the specific claim it is attached to; no citation rests on the model's assurance alone (guards against fabricated citations, §9.6; cf. Chapter 5).
- **Every claim carries a justifiable certainty flag.** Substantive claims state their confidence; any claim the author cannot personally stand behind is weakened or removed (guards against over-claiming, §9.6).
- **The author has read every sentence they are signing.** Agent-drafted prose has been interrogated for warranted substance, not accepted for fluency (guards against style laundering, §9.6).
- **The disclosure statement is generated from provenance and matches the venue's class.** The record of which agent did which task feeds a disclosure at the required granularity; confidential manuscripts under review were kept off unsanctioned external systems (satisfies policy, §9.4; cf. Chapter 12).
- **No agent is listed or implied as an author.** Authorship and accountability rest with the human authors alone (§9.4).
