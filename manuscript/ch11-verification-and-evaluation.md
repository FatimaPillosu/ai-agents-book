## 11.1 Why a leaderboard is the wrong instrument

The question a benchmark answers is not the question a working scientist needs answered, and conflating the two is the most common way that trust in an agentic workflow is misplaced before any output is produced. Public leaderboards measure general capability: they report how a model or an agent performs on a fixed, shared task distribution — resolving issues in open-source repositories, answering examination-style questions, completing curated coding problems — and they are indispensable for tracking the field's trajectory, as the coarse figures cited in Chapter 1 were used to do. What they cannot measure is fitness for a specific workflow, on specific data, under a specific institution's tolerance for error, because none of those particulars is present in the benchmark. A model that resolves a high fraction of software-engineering tasks in the aggregate tells you almost nothing about whether an agent built around it will correctly regrid a particular ensemble forecast, reconcile a particular gauge network, or preserve units through a particular pipeline; the aggregate is a property of the distribution, and your task is a single point that may sit anywhere relative to it. The jagged frontier described in Chapter 1 is precisely the reason the aggregate does not transfer: reliability tracks how densely a task is represented in training material rather than its apparent difficulty, so a high mean score coexists with severe, task-specific failures that the mean conceals. The implication for practice is that a benchmark result is admissible as evidence about capability in general and inadmissible as evidence about a particular workflow's correctness. This is not a counsel of despair about measurement; it is an argument for measuring the right thing. The right thing is the workflow's performance on the actual task, with the actual data, at the actual scale intended — task-grounded evaluation — and the remainder of this chapter sets out how to structure that evaluation so that a claim of correctness carries evidential weight proportionate to the decision resting on it.

The environmental sciences have an unusually mature literature on exactly this problem, developed over decades for physically based models long before agents existed, and it transfers with surprisingly little modification. Hydrological modelling in particular has confronted the fact that a model can reproduce a calibration record faithfully and still be right for the wrong reasons — and the discipline's response was to formalise what counts as evidence that a model is fit for a stated purpose (Klemeš [verify]; Refsgaard & Henriksen [verify]). The computational-science literature developed a parallel and complementary vocabulary, separating the question of whether the code solves the equations correctly from the question of whether the equations describe reality (Oberkampf & Trucano [verify]), while systematic accounts of the modelling process itself set out the iterative steps by which a model earns confidence (Jakeman et al. [verify]). None of these frameworks was written with language models in mind, and each must be adapted rather than transplanted, because an agent's failure modes — plausible, fluent and uncorrelated with correctness — differ from the failure modes of a numerical scheme. The adaptation this chapter proposes is a five-tier hierarchy of evidential strength for claims about an agentic workflow's output, in which each tier is defined operationally by the check that establishes it, and higher tiers subsume lower ones. My confidence that the hierarchy is useful is high; my confidence that any particular tier boundary is drawn in exactly the right place is moderate, and I mark where the author's own judgement should settle the definitions.

## 11.2 Five tiers of evidence

The five tiers form an ascending ladder in which each rung answers a stronger question than the one below it, and a claim's evidential strength is the highest tier it has actually passed rather than the highest it could in principle reach. **[AUTHOR: confirm tier definitions — the labels and boundaries below are a proposal adapted from the model-evaluation literature; adjust so they match the terminology you intend to standardise across the book.]** The lowest tier, **execution**, asks only whether the workflow ran to completion and produced output of the expected form; the check that establishes it is that the process terminates without error and the output conforms to its declared schema — the right number of fields, the right types, values within admissible ranges. Execution is necessary and almost worthless on its own, because a workflow that produces a well-formed, entirely wrong answer passes it cleanly; its value is as a gate that catches the crudest failures — the crash, the empty file, the truncated field — at negligible cost. The second tier, **internal consistency**, asks whether the output is coherent with itself and with its inputs under constraints that must hold regardless of the correct answer; the check is that domain invariants are satisfied — mass and energy balance where they apply, monotonicity where it is required, units carried correctly end to end, totals reconciling with their parts. Internal consistency catches a large and important class of silent errors, the unit slip and the conservation violation among them, precisely the errors Chapter 13 catalogues; but it too can be satisfied by an output that is self-consistent and false, so it does not establish correctness, only the absence of a specific family of incoherence.

The third tier, **reproduction of held-out truth**, is the first at which the word correct is earned, and it is the pivot of the whole hierarchy. The tier asks whether the workflow reproduces a reference value that it did not have access to when it ran, and the check that establishes it is a split-sample test: a portion of ground truth is withheld, the workflow is run without sight of it, and its output is compared against the withheld reference by a metric fixed in advance. The discipline here is the discipline hydrological modelling settled on long ago — evaluation against data not used in fitting — and its force depends entirely on the withholding being genuine, which for an agent means the reference must be absent not only from the immediate inputs but from any material the model could have seen, a stronger and subtler condition addressed in §11.3. The fourth tier, **out-of-sample generalisation**, asks the harder question of whether the workflow holds up under conditions materially different from those in which it was developed, and the check is the differential test drawn directly from Klemeš's hierarchical testing scheme [verify]: the workflow is evaluated on a regime it was not tuned for — a different catchment, a different season, a wetter or drier period, a different instrument — and judged by whether performance degrades gracefully or collapses. A workflow that reproduces held-out truth within its development regime but fails under transfer is fit for interpolation and unfit for extrapolation, and the fourth tier is what distinguishes the two. The fifth and highest tier, **independent adversarial scrutiny**, asks whether the claim survives a competent party actively trying to break it; the check is that an independent reviewer — a human, or a separate agent constituted for the purpose as in Chapters 10 and 12 — attempts to falsify the result, probes its edge cases and alternative explanations, and fails to overturn it. This tier is expensive and cannot be automated away, because it is the point at which judgement, not computation, certifies the result; it is also the only tier that offers real protection against errors no pre-specified check anticipated. The tiers are cumulative: a Tier 4 claim has passed Tiers 1 through 4, and citing a tier is a claim about evidence actually gathered, not effort intended.

## 11.3 Verification must be external to the system verified

The single principle that holds the hierarchy together is that verification must be external to the system being verified, a point made in Chapter 1 and load-bearing enough to restate as the governing rule of this chapter. Language models are poor judges of their own correctness: a model asked whether its output is right will answer fluently and with a confidence uncorrelated with the truth of the answer, for the same reason its errors are plausible in the first place — fluency and correctness are produced by different mechanisms and come apart under exactly the conditions where verification matters most (high confidence in the pattern; the magnitude of the miscalibration varies by model and task [verify]). The operational consequence is that no tier of the hierarchy may be established by the agent that produced the output, nor by an identical agent, nor by the same model prompted to check itself, because all three share the failure that verification is meant to catch. Each check must therefore derive its authority from a source the generating agent does not control: the schema at Tier 1 is fixed by the workflow's designer, not proposed by the agent at run time; the invariants at Tier 2 are physical or definitional facts external to the model; the held-out reference at Tier 3 is data the agent never saw; the transfer regime at Tier 4 is chosen by the evaluator to be unlike the development regime; and the reviewer at Tier 5 is independent by construction. Where a check is implemented in code — a test suite, a schema validator, a mass-balance assertion — that code is itself an external artefact, written and reviewed under the governance of Chapters 7 and 12, and not generated and graded by the same agent in one unexamined step.

Externality is easier to state than to secure, and the subtlest threat to it is contamination of the held-out reference. A split-sample test establishes nothing if the withheld data reached the model by another route: if the reference values appear in the agent's context, in a file it retrieved, in a cached result from a prior run, or — harder to exclude — in the public material the underlying model was trained on, then the test measures recall rather than reproduction, and reports a correctness the workflow does not possess. This concern has no exact counterpart in classical model evaluation, where the model cannot have memorised the validation record, and it is the principal way the adaptation from the hydrological literature must go beyond its source. Guarding against it requires provenance discipline of the kind Chapter 12 specifies: knowing what entered the agent's context, recording where each reference came from, and preferring, for the highest-stakes claims, held-out data generated after the model's training cut-off or held under conditions that make prior exposure implausible. The limitation to acknowledge is that contamination can rarely be excluded with certainty for a closed model whose training corpus is unknown; the honest position is to treat freedom from contamination as a claim with its own confidence level, to raise that confidence by controlling the routes you can control, and to weight a Tier 3 result accordingly rather than treating the number at face value.

## 11.4 Operating the ladder in practice

The hierarchy is a tool for allocating verification effort deliberately, not a demand that every output climb to the top, and using it well means matching the tier attained to the stakes of the decision the output informs. Climbing the ladder is not free, and the cost structure was set out in Chapter 4: Tiers 1 and 2 are cheap and largely automatable and should be applied to essentially everything, since a schema check and an invariant assertion cost little and catch the crudest and some of the most dangerous failures; Tier 3 costs whatever it costs to hold out and curate reference data and is warranted wherever a quantitative result will be reported or acted upon; Tier 4 costs the design of a genuine transfer test and is warranted wherever the workflow will be applied outside the regime it was built in, which for operational environmental work is the common case rather than the exception; and Tier 5 costs scarce expert attention and is reserved for claims whose failure would be consequential — a result headed for publication, a warning informing a decision, a configuration about to enter routine operation. A workflow that stops at Tier 2 for an exploratory triage is properly governed; the same workflow stopping at Tier 2 for a published result is not. The tier reached should be recorded alongside the output as part of its provenance (Chapter 12), so that a later reader — a reviewer, a successor, the author six months on — can see not merely what the workflow concluded but how strong the evidence for that conclusion was, and can decide whether the tier attained matches the use now being made of it. This record is also what makes the failure gallery of Chapter 13 legible: nearly every failure in that chapter is an output trusted at a tier above the one its evidence actually supported, and the discipline of naming the tier is the discipline that prevents it. The limitation worth stating plainly is that the hierarchy governs evidential strength, not importance: it can tell you how well established a claim is, and it cannot tell you whether the claim is the one that matters, which remains a matter of scientific judgement that no tier, and no agent, can supply. **[AUTHOR: a worked instance from your own practice — a claim you took to Tier 3 or 4, the check you used, and what it did or did not catch — would ground this section and connect it to the case studies in Part IV.]**
