# Theme 1 — Agentic AI architectures and autonomy (ch01, ch02, ch16, ch17)

**Anthropic (Favaro, M. and Clark, J., with editorial support from Ruiz, S.) (2026). "When AI builds itself." The Anthropic Institute.** https://www.anthropic.com/institute/recursive-self-improvement

This Anthropic Institute report is the single most consequential 2026 primary source located on agentic self-improvement, and the book should treat it as a milestone-and-warning pair in the same register as "The AI Scientist" (theme 6, below). Anthropic's central claim is that its own engineering organisation has crossed a threshold: as of May 2026, over 80% of the code merged into Anthropic's production codebase originated from Claude, engineers shipped roughly eight times as much code per quarter in Q2 2026 as in 2024, and the duration of tasks Claude can complete autonomously has been doubling roughly every four months — faster than the previously reported seven-month doubling time, and independently corroborated by METR's contemporaneous update (below). The report also gives research-specific findings: on SWE-bench, performance moved from single-digit to near-saturated scores over two years; on CORE-Bench (research reproduction), from roughly 20% to near-saturation in fifteen months; and on open-ended research judgement tasks, Claude's next-step choices were preferred over humans' 64% of the time by April 2026. Methodologically, Anthropic combined public benchmarks, internal telemetry, employee surveys and qualitative session review — self-reported, single-vendor evidence the book must flag as such. Anthropic's own stated caveats are unusually candid and worth quoting: "lines of code is an imperfect measure" of productivity, employee self-estimates of gain are likely inflated, and research-autonomy demonstrations "didn't transfer cleanly to production-scale models." The report explicitly maps a path towards recursive self-improvement (AI systems designing their successors) and calls for the option of a coordinated, verifiable industry-wide pause, while acknowledging verification is "much more challenging" than existing arms-control precedents because training runs are far easier to conceal than physical infrastructure. For the book: ch01's capability timeline needs this as its most recent data point; ch16/ch17 should use the source's self-reported, incentive-laden nature as a teaching example of why capability claims from an AI vendor about its own AI need the same independent-verification treatment the book already prescribes for scientific claims (ch11). Caveats: single organisation, self-reported, commercially interested in demonstrating capability; the exact publication date carries minor uncertainty — the page's content is dated to May 2026 data, secondary reporting places formal publication on 5 June 2026 **[verify exact day]**.

**Arunkumar, V., Gangadharan, G.R. and Buyya, R. (2026). "Agentic Artificial Intelligence (AI): Architectures, Taxonomies, and Evaluation of Large Language Model Agents." arXiv preprint.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.12560

This January 2026 preprint proposes a unified taxonomy for agentic systems organised around six dimensions — Perception, Brain, Planning, Action, Tool Use and Collaboration — and traces the field's trajectory from linear, single-pass reasoning to native inference-time reasoning models, plus the standardisation wave around the Model Context Protocol and native computer-use interfaces. Rajkumar Buyya's involvement (a senior distributed-systems researcher) gives the taxonomy an engineering-systems flavour rather than a purely cognitive-science one, distinguishing it from the CoALA framework (Sumers et al., first sweep) which draws on cognitive-architecture history instead. The paper explicitly names hallucination during action execution, infinite-loop risk and prompt injection as the field's central open challenges, situating governance concerns as core to the taxonomy rather than an afterthought. For the book, this is a useful 2026 cross-check for ch02's anatomy figure: writers should verify that the loop/tools/context/orchestration structure the book already uses maps sensibly onto this newer six-dimension scheme (Collaboration corresponds to ch10's multi-agent material; Tool Use and Action correspond to ch02's core loop), letting the book note that independent 2026 taxonomies continue to converge on broadly the same structural decomposition as CoALA (2023) and the book's own anatomy — strengthening ch02's claim to reflect settled structure rather than one author's idiosyncratic framing. Caveats: unreviewed arXiv preprint with no author institutional affiliations confirmed from the abstract page **[verify]**; like the first sweep's Wang et al. survey, it is a synthesis rather than an empirical study, so it should be cited for taxonomic vocabulary and field-trajectory framing, not for any specific capability or safety claim; and, being a survey of a fast-moving field, some of its "emerging" standardisation claims (e.g. MCP's status) will likely already be dated by the time of citation and should be kept to approximate years in print per the vendor-neutral rule.

**METR (2026). "Time Horizon 1.1." METR research blog, 29 January 2026.** https://metr.org/blog/2026-1-29-time-horizon-1-1/

METR (Model Evaluation and Threat Research) is the organisation behind the widely cited "task-length doubling" measure of agent capability: the duration of software and reasoning tasks frontier models can complete autonomously at a 50% success rate, tracked from 2019 to the present. This January 2026 update revises METR's own headline finding. The original methodology (published March 2025, not separately verified this sweep) reported a roughly seven-month doubling time from 2019 to 2025; Time Horizon 1.1 reports the trend accelerated sharply from 2024 onward, with a doubling time from 2023 of approximately 128.7 days (just over four months, 90% CI [105, 157] days) and from 2024 of approximately 89 days (roughly three months) — figures that independently corroborate the four-month doubling rate Anthropic reports in "When AI builds itself" (above) via an entirely different measurement approach, strengthening confidence in the underlying trend even though both organisations have interests in demonstrating rapid progress. The measure correlates task length with success rate at R² = 0.83 across roughly 230 tasks, predominantly software-engineering with some general-reasoning tasks included. For the book, this is the strongest available quantitative anchor for ch01's capability-timeline figure and for ch16's planning horizon (if autonomous task length keeps doubling every three to four months, an on-ramp built around today's task-length ceiling will be obsolete within a single budget cycle — an argument ch16 can make explicitly). It is also methodologically instructive for ch11: METR's approach — a fixed, pre-registered task suite, repeated over time, with a single comparable metric — is itself a task-grounded evaluation design the chapter can point to as exemplary, in contrast to leaderboard benchmarks that change composition between rounds. Caveats: the task suite is coding- and reasoning-heavy and may not transfer to interpretive scientific workflows; extrapolating exponential trends is inherently uncertain (METR states this explicitly); and, as an organisation focused on AI risk evaluation, METR's framing of "task horizon" as the key capability metric is one methodological choice among several, not a consensus standard.

# Theme 2 — Verification and evaluation of agents (ch11 — deepest coverage; also ch07, ch10, ch13)

*Ordered: judge validity and bias first, then benchmark auditing, then agent-specific failure/uncertainty work.*

**Norman, J.D., Rivera, M.U. and Hughes, D.A. (2026). "Reliability without Validity: A Systematic, Large-Scale Evaluation of LLM-as-a-Judge Models Across Agreement, Consistency, and Bias." arXiv preprint.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19544

This June 2026 preprint is the deepest and most consequential 2026 addition to ch11's judge-validity evidence base, and its title states the finding the book most needs: reliability and validity are different properties, and a judge can have one without the other. The authors evaluated 21 judge models from nine providers across three established benchmarks (MT-Bench, JudgeBench, RewardBench) using three complementary protocols — agreement against human labels, test-retest consistency, and bias auditing — over roughly 541,000 individual judgements across 118 evaluation runs, by far the largest such study located in either sweep. Four findings matter for the book. First, "agreement deflation": exact-match agreement figures (the kind of number the first sweep flagged as needing caveats, e.g. Zheng et al.'s 85%) overstate true agreement by 33–41 percentage points once measured properly with Cohen's kappa, a chance-corrected statistic — a direct, quantified revision of how ch11 should present headline judge-agreement numbers. Second, judge rankings are unstable, shifting by up to 14 positions depending which benchmark is used to rank them, undermining any claim that one judge model is simply "the best." Third — the paper's title finding — two production-deployed judges showed high test-retest reliability (>0.95, meaning the judge is highly self-consistent) coexisting with severe position bias (>0.10), demonstrating a judge can be dependably wrong in a fixed direction: consistency is not correctness, a distinction ch11's verification checklist should state explicitly, since a naive reliability check (does the judge agree with itself?) would falsely certify a biased gate as trustworthy. Fourth, verbosity bias was small under a single pairwise rubric (<0.011), qualifying rather than contradicting the sycophancy/verbosity literature in the first sweep by showing bias magnitude depends heavily on evaluation-protocol design. The authors propose a "Minimum Viable Validation Protocol" for practitioners, which ch11 could adapt directly into its own gate-measurement guidance. Caveats: unreviewed preprint; benchmarks are general chat/QA tasks, not scientific artefacts, so magnitudes will differ for domain review gates; author institutional affiliations not confirmed from the abstract page **[verify]**.

**Wang, J., Bianchi, F., Zhu, S., Nie, F., Kwon, Y., Dhingra, B. and Zou, J. (2026). "Automated Benchmark Auditing for AI Agents and Large Language Models." arXiv preprint.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26079

This May 2026 preprint directly extends the first sweep's Zhu et al. (2025) "Agentic Benchmark Checklist" — flagged there as validated on only one case study — by automating the audit process and running it at scale. The authors built Auto Benchmark Audit (ABA), an agentic system that systematically inspects individual benchmark tasks for three defect classes: hidden environment dependencies, specification gaps, and limited or incorrect grading logic. Applied to 168 benchmarks across nine domains, ABA found problems — ambiguous task design, environment conflicts or incorrect ground truth — in over 25.7% of tasks; removing the flawed tasks materially changed reported rankings and raised average scores on two widely used agentic benchmarks (SWE-bench Verified and Terminal-Bench 2) by roughly 9.9 and 9.6 percentage points respectively once the noise was stripped out. This is exactly the scaling-up the first sweep's coverage-limits note implicitly called for: where Zhu et al. showed manual auditing works on one benchmark, this paper shows the audit itself can be automated and applied broadly, with results in the same direction — benchmark scores are inflated by defects, and the inflation is large and quantifiable. For ch11, this source supplies a second, independent demonstration of the chapter's central claim that "automated verification is itself an instrument with a measurable error rate," now with a concrete method a research group could adapt: an agent that audits another agent's evaluation harness before anyone trusts its pass rate. It also strengthens ch13's failure-gallery entry on over-agreeable or defective gates, since a 25.7% task-defect rate is a specific, citable number rather than an anecdote. Caveats: preprint, not yet peer-reviewed **[verify venue]**; the audited benchmarks are software/agentic-tooling benchmarks, so the defect rate is domain-specific and should not be presented as a universal figure; the authors release their own tool and annotations, a mild incentive to demonstrate the tool's value the book should note in passing.

**Mehta, A. (2026). "Confident and Wrong: Silent Semantic Failures in Coding Agents." arXiv preprint.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25764

This March 2026 single-author preprint gives the book a precisely quantified, citable example of the "silent failure" pattern that recurs independently across this sweep's grey literature (see the YouTube companion report's Nate Jones material on harness maintenance, which names the identical phenomenon from practitioner experience). The author distinguishes submission rate (did the agent produce and submit a patch?) from test-verified resolution rate (did the patch actually pass the repository's held-out tests?) across 1,750 agent trajectories on 50 SWE-bench Verified tasks, comparing four frontier models. The gap is stark and model-dependent: GPT-5 submitted a patch 100% of the time but resolved the issue only 44% of the time; Llama 4 submitted 99% of the time but resolved only 18%; Gemini submitted less often (70%) but resolved slightly more of what it did submit (50%). Crucially, these silent semantic failures — confidently wrong, but consistent across repeated runs — dominate each model's failure profile (80% of Llama 4's failures, 68% of GPT-5's failures), meaning the two cheap monitoring signals a practitioner might reach for by default — "did it finish?" and "does it give the same answer twice?" — look healthy at exactly the moment the output cannot be trusted. This directly elaborates the first sweep's SWE-bench entry (which flagged execution-grounded gates as having their own measurable false-negative rates) and belongs alongside it in ch11, while its concrete numbers make it one of the strongest available exhibits for ch13's failure gallery and for ch07's insistence on independent, execution-based review of coding-agent output rather than trusting completion signals. The author's policy recommendation — reward systems that recognise when abstention is the correct output, rather than rewarding confident completion — is directly relevant to ch03's stop-condition guidance. Caveats: single-author preprint, not peer-reviewed, no institutional affiliation confirmed **[verify]**; SWE-bench-only, so specific percentages are coding-domain figures and should not be generalised numerically to scientific-workflow agents, only structurally.

**Oh, C., Park, S., Kim, T.E., Li, J., Li, W., Yeh, S., Du, X., Hassani, H., Bogdan, P., Song, D. and Li, S. (2026). "Uncertainty Quantification in LLM Agents: Foundations, Emerging Challenges, and Opportunities." arXiv preprint; accepted to the ACL 2026 main conference.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05073

This February 2026 survey (revised through April 2026, accepted to ACL 2026's main conference — unusually for this list, therefore peer-reviewed by the time of citation) brings an entirely new methodological angle to the book's verification chapter: uncertainty quantification (UQ) applied specifically to agents rather than single-turn question answering. The authors argue most existing UQ research assumes a static, single-response setting that does not transfer to agents that act, observe and revise plans over many steps, then set out a general formulation covering existing agent-UQ approaches, four open technical challenges (choosing appropriate uncertainty estimators for agentic settings, handling heterogeneous entity types such as tool calls versus natural-language reasoning, tracking how uncertainty shifts across an interactive trajectory, and building fine-grained, scalable evaluation benchmarks for it), and a numerical illustration on the τ²-bench agent benchmark (a successor to τ-bench, already in the first sweep). For ch11, this source is valuable less for a specific finding than for a vocabulary and research direction the book should acknowledge honestly: a confidence-scored agent output ("I am 80% sure this QC flag is correct") is a different, and on the evidence here less mature, capability than a pass/fail verification gate, and the chapter should not imply the two are interchangeable. It also gives ch13 a principled way to describe one specific failure type — an agent that is wrong and does not know it is wrong — as a UQ failure distinct from a verification-gate failure (the gate can be excellent and still be defeated if the agent never flags the case for review). Caveats: as a survey and position paper, it catalogues open problems rather than solved ones — the book should not cite it for any working UQ method's performance; and the field it surveys is explicitly early-stage, so any UQ technique named here is more likely than most 2026 sources in this report to be superseded within the book's own writing window.

**Soumik, S.K. (2026). "Judging the Judges: A Systematic Evaluation of Bias Mitigation Strategies in LLM-as-a-Judge Pipelines." arXiv preprint.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23178

Despite the near-identical title, this April 2026 preprint is a different paper from Shi et al.'s 2024 "Judging the Judges" already in the first sweep (which measured position bias at scale); this one moves from diagnosis to mitigation, and adds a bias type the first sweep's sources did not name. The author tested five judge models spanning Google, Anthropic, OpenAI and Meta against nine distinct debiasing strategies across three benchmark datasets (roughly 975 evaluation pairs), examining four bias categories. The headline finding: style bias — a judge's preference for markdown-formatted answers over plain prose — is the dominant bias measured (0.10–0.76 depending on model), larger than the position bias the field has focused on since Zheng et al. (2023, first sweep); this is a genuinely new and actionable finding for ch11, since it implies a reviewer agent could be gamed, deliberately or accidentally, simply by formatting an otherwise weak scientific summary with headers and bullet points. The paper also found verbosity preferences heterogeneous and model-specific (some models preferred longer answers, Claude models preferred shorter ones), but that all tested models correctly preferred complete over truncated answers once truncation itself was controlled for — a useful nuance against over-generalising the first sweep's verbosity-bias finding. On mitigation, a cost-conscious result stands out: a mid-tier model (Gemini 2.5 Flash) combined with appropriate debiasing techniques reached 71.0% agreement with human judgements at roughly one-fifteenth the inference cost of top-tier alternatives, directly supporting ch11 and ch16's shared argument (echoing Kapoor et al., first sweep) that accuracy-only judge selection ignores a cost axis that can be optimised without much accuracy loss. For the book: ch11 should present style bias as an addition to its list of characterised judge biases, and the cost-mitigation finding strengthens ch16's cost-model section. Caveats: unreviewed preprint, single author; the four bias categories and nine mitigation strategies tested are the author's own selection, not a field-standard set, so completeness should not be assumed.

# Theme 3 — Multi-agent failure taxonomies (ch10, ch13)

**Rafi, M.N., Ahasanuzzaman, M., Kim, D.J., Wang, Z. and Chen, T.-H. (2026). "FALAT: Tracing Failures in LLM Agent Trajectories via Dependency-Guided Search." arXiv preprint.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00765

This May 2026 preprint moves the multi-agent failure literature from taxonomy (naming failure categories, as MAST does — first sweep) towards diagnosis (finding which specific step in a long trajectory actually caused a failure, when later steps merely inherited an earlier corrupted state). The authors' framing captures a problem ch13's failure gallery should state explicitly: "later actions may appear incorrect, but only because they depend on an earlier corrupted state," meaning a naive post-hoc review that looks at the point of visible failure will often blame the wrong agent or step. FALAT works by building an expectation of correct execution to flag suspicious regions of a trajectory, mapping dependencies among agent decisions, tool outputs and inter-agent messages to separate error-originating steps from steps that only propagated an earlier mistake, then testing candidate fixes by checking whether correcting a candidate step restores the expected outcome. On a mix of algorithmically generated and hand-crafted failure trajectories, FALAT achieved 46.0% and 29.1% step-level attribution accuracy respectively, outperforming specialised attribution baselines and direct LLM-prompting approaches to the same problem — modest absolute numbers that honestly convey how hard failure attribution in long agent trajectories still is, even with a purpose-built method. For the book, this is a useful, concrete addition to ch10's roster design and ch13's gallery: it gives a name and a method to the intuition that "the human reviewing a failed multi-agent run needs to find the actual cause, not just the visible symptom" and quantifies why that is a genuinely hard, unsolved problem rather than a matter of reviewer diligence. Caveats: single preprint, unreviewed; the abstract makes no explicit reference to MAST or other taxonomies, so its relationship to the first sweep's Cemri et al. work is inferred by the book, not stated by the authors **[verify by reading the full paper if a direct textual link is wanted]**; accuracy in the 30–46% range means the method should be presented as a promising research direction, not a deployable tool.

**Tang, L., Mei, J., Liu, D., Qian, C., Cheng, D., Shao, J. and Hu, X. (2026). "Interpreting Emergent Extreme Events in Multi-Agent Systems." arXiv preprint.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20538

This January 2026 preprint addresses a failure category the first sweep's MAST taxonomy does not centre: not a single agent's mistake, but a high-impact, unexpected outcome that emerges only from the interaction dynamics of multiple agents together, with no single step obviously at fault. The authors build an attribution framework using adapted Shapley values (a method from cooperative game theory for fairly dividing "credit" among contributors) to trace an emergent extreme event back to contributions from individual agent actions across time, answering when an event originated, which agents drove it, and which specific behaviours contributed, aggregated across temporal, agent-level and behavioural dimensions. They demonstrate the framework on economic, financial and social multi-agent simulations rather than scientific or engineering workflows. For the book, this paper's relevance is conceptual rather than a source of directly transferable numbers: ch10's discussion of consensus and correlated failure (already grounded in the first sweep's multiagent-debate paper, which flagged that debating similar models can converge on a shared error) gains a second, independently developed vocabulary for the same underlying concern — that multi-agent systems can produce collective behaviour no individual agent's transcript explains, precisely the kind of failure a per-agent review checklist would miss. Ch13's failure gallery could use this as a named category — "emergent/collective failure," distinct from the specification, misalignment and verification categories MAST already supplies — provided the book is honest that the evidence comes from abstract simulated-economy settings, not anything resembling an environmental-science pipeline. Caveats: unreviewed preprint; the application domains (economic/financial/social simulation) are far from the book's hydrology and meteorology worked examples, so this should be cited for the conceptual category it names, not as domain evidence; Shapley-value attribution is computationally expensive, a practical limitation the abstract does not quantify.

# Theme 4 — Prompt injection and agent security: 2026 standards and guidance (ch12)

**National Cyber Security Centre (UK), Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (US), National Security Agency (US), Australian Signals Directorate's Australian Cyber Security Centre, Canadian Centre for Cyber Security and National Cyber Security Centre New Zealand (2026). "Careful Adoption of Agentic AI Services" (joint advisory, dated 30 April 2026) and "Thinking carefully before adopting agentic AI" (NCSC-UK companion blog, 15 May 2026).** NCSC blog (fetched and verified directly): https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blogs/thinking-carefully-before-adopting-agentic-ai · joint advisory PDF: https://media.defense.gov/2026/Apr/30/2003922823/-1/-1/0/CAREFUL%20ADOPTION%20OF%20AGENTIC%20AI%20SERVICES_FINAL.PDF

This is the first-ever coordinated Five Eyes security guidance on agentic AI specifically, and it directly fills a gap the first sweep named explicitly ("UK-institution security guidance (NCSC)... not swept this pass"). Six national cyber agencies — NCSC-UK, the US CISA and NSA, Australia's ACSC, Canada's CCCS and New Zealand's NCSC-NZ — jointly published a detailed advisory (dated 30 April 2026 on the source PDF; widely reported as released 1 May 2026), with NCSC-UK's own companion blog post — fetched and verified directly this sweep — following on 15 May 2026. The joint advisory catalogues 23 distinct risks across five categories (privilege, design and configuration, behavioural, structural, and accountability) with over 100 associated best practices; the NCSC-UK blog distils this into practitioner-facing guidance the book can cite with confidence. Key recommendations, confirmed from the fetched page: begin with tightly bounded pilots on low-risk, clearly defined tasks before expanding scope; apply least-privilege access, time-limited scope and temporary rather than long-lived credentials; implement continuous behaviour monitoring and pre-deployment threat modelling; and, most quotable for the book, establish clear, named human accountability before deployment, because "if you cannot understand, monitor or contain an agent's actions, it is not ready for deployment." This maps almost exactly onto ch12's existing structure (least-privilege tool access, audit trails, what institutional IT will ask) and gives it an authoritative, very recent, multi-national citation to sit alongside the first sweep's NIST AI 600-1 and OWASP sources; the "start small, low-risk pilots" recommendation also directly supports ch16's on-ramp philosophy, and the incremental-autonomy framing usefully informs ch04's decision flowchart. # Theme 5 — Hallucination and fabricated citations: 2026 evidence (ch05, ch09, ch13)

**Ansari, M.S. (2026). "Compound Deception in Elite Peer Review: A Failure Mode Taxonomy of 100 Fabricated Citations at NeurIPS 2025." arXiv preprint.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05930

This February 2026 preprint is the most striking update located to the first sweep's fabricated-citation evidence base, because its evidence comes not from prompting a chatbot and checking its output (the first sweep's Walters & Wilder and Cabezas-Clavijo studies) but from papers that already passed expert peer review at one of the most competitive venues in machine learning. The author examined 100 fabricated citations found across 53 papers accepted at NeurIPS 2025 — roughly 1% of all accepted submissions, despite each paper having been read by three to five expert reviewers — and built a taxonomy of five failure modes: Total Fabrication (66% of cases, a citation to a non-existent work), Partial Attribute Corruption (27%, a real paper cited with wrong details), Identifier Hijacking (4%, a fabricated citation dressed with a real but mismatched DOI or arXiv identifier), Placeholder Hallucination (2%) and Semantic Hallucination (1%, a real citation used to support a claim it does not actually make). The paper's most important finding for the book is that 100% of the examined hallucinations combined multiple deception mechanisms simultaneously — bimodally, 92% of contaminated papers had only one or two hallucinated citations, i.e. a small, easy-to-miss contamination, not a paper riddled with obvious fakes — which is precisely why the mechanical, tool-executed citation verification ch05 already teaches (resolve every DOI, match every title) cannot be replaced by a human plausibility read, however expert: elite peer review, with multiple expert readers, still missed these. For ch05, ch09 and ch13, this is the strongest available evidence that citation fabrication survives even the most rigorous conventional human review process available in the field, directly reinforcing and updating with harder evidence the first sweep's Walters & Wilder finding that fabrications "look legitimate at first glance." The author's proposed remedy — mandatory automated citation verification at submission — is a policy recommendation ch09 could cite as an emerging norm. Caveats: single-author preprint, not yet peer-reviewed itself (an evident irony worth naming in prose); the NeurIPS-specific 1% contamination rate is a single-venue, single-year figure and should not be presented as a general base rate across all peer review; no institutional affiliation confirmed from the abstract page **[verify]**.

**Rao, D. and Callison-Burch, C. (2026). "BibTeX Citation Hallucinations in Scientific Publishing Agents: Evaluation and Mitigation." arXiv preprint.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03159

This April 2026 preprint is valuable to the book chiefly for its mitigation evidence, which the first sweep's citation-fabrication sources lacked. The authors built a benchmark of 931 papers across four scientific domains and tested how accurately three frontier models (GPT-5, Claude Sonnet-4.6, Gemini-3 Flash) produce correct BibTeX entries — the standard machine-readable citation format researchers paste directly into manuscripts, a more operationally realistic test than free-text citation generation. Baseline accuracy across models was 83.6% at the level of individual fields, but only 50.9% of entries were fully correct in every field simultaneously — a useful distinction for ch05, since a citation with one wrong field (a swapped page number, a wrong year) is arguably more dangerous than an obviously fabricated one, because it passes a casual glance. Accuracy dropped sharply (by 27.7 percentage points) for recent papers compared with well-known ones, indicating the models were relying on memorised (parametric) knowledge of famous papers rather than actually using their search tools even when search access was available — a concrete, quantified instance of the first sweep's general point that retrieval access does not guarantee retrieval use. On mitigation, the authors tested clibib, an open-source tool that resolves citations deterministically against the Zotero Translation Server and CrossRef, used as a second-stage correction of the model's initial draft citation; this two-stage approach raised overall accuracy to 91.5% and fully correct entries to 78.3%, while cutting the rate of the tool introducing new errors (regressions) to 0.8%, versus 4.8% for a cruder single-stage integration. For ch05, this is exactly the "citation verification as a mechanical, tool-executed step" the chapter already prescribes, now with a working open-source implementation and quantified evidence that the design of the verification step (two-stage versus one-stage) materially affects how much residual risk remains. Caveats: preprint, not yet peer-reviewed; four domains only, and BibTeX-specific, so findings may not transfer directly to other citation formats or to narrative literature synthesis; Callison-Burch's institutional affiliation (University of Pennsylvania, per general knowledge of the author) was not independently confirmed from the abstract page itself this sweep **[verify]**.

# Theme 8 — Policy on AI in research and publishing (ch09, ch16)

**Elsevier (2026). "Generative AI policies for journals." Elsevier editorial policy page, updated June 2026.** https://www.elsevier.com/about/policies-and-standards/generative-ai-policies-for-journals

This is Elsevier's own policy page, directly fetched and verified this sweep, updated June 2026 (exact day not shown on the page) and therefore the most current major-publisher AI policy statement located to sit alongside the first sweep's Nature editorial (2023) and its current Nature Portfolio policy. The substance closely mirrors what the first sweep already found — authorship is confined to humans ("authorship implies responsibilities and tasks that can only be attributed to and performed by humans"), each human author remains accountable for the accuracy and integrity of the whole work, and reviewers are barred from uploading manuscript material to generative AI tools because doing so risks breaching peer review's confidentiality obligations — but adds two details useful for ch09's practical guidance. **Slimi, Z. (2026). "A systematic critical review of generative AI's impact on authorship, pedagogy, and integrity (2023–2025)." Frontiers in Education, 11.** DOI: 10.3389/feduc.2026.1769680

Published 29 April 2026, this is a properly peer-reviewed, methodologically rigorous systematic review — a stronger evidentiary form than the first sweep's journalistic Nature survey piece on similar territory — and gives ch09 an academic anchor for its claim that AI-in-publishing policy is fragmented and unsettled rather than converging. The author searched Scopus, ERIC and IEEE Xplore (database search completed 1 December 2025), screened 117 records down to 60 final sources (54 peer-reviewed studies from 2023–2025 plus six international policy documents), and applied quality appraisal (adapted CASP and MMAT criteria) and NVivo-assisted thematic synthesis, with external audit validation on a sample of sources — a considerably more rigorous method than either sweep's other policy-survey sources, which are journalism or institutional documents rather than academic reviews. Four themes emerged. On authorship, formal policies universally prohibit AI co-authorship, yet the review finds authorship practice "being fundamentally reconstituted as tacit, hybrid, human–AI collaboration" underneath that formal prohibition — disclosure rates vary enormously by discipline (70% in nursing journals versus 46.7% in plastic surgery, in the studies reviewed), a concrete illustration of the adoption-outrunning-disclosure gap the first sweep's Nature survey also found. On pedagogy, the field has shifted from a "defensive," detection-focused paradigm towards redesigning assessment itself. On integrity governance, two unresolved competing models persist: technological policing (detection tools, found inconsistently effective) versus a pedagogical-trust approach favoured by education researchers. A fourth theme raises algorithmic-bias and epistemic-justice concerns, including an "equity paradox" where generative AI simultaneously helps and further stratifies unequal access to research capacity. The review's overall conclusion — that "prevailing, reactive policy frameworks are inadequate" and proactive, equity-focused governance is needed — is a strong, academically sourced parallel to the book's own governance-first thesis, applicable to ch09's disclosure discussion and ch16's institutional-adoption section. Caveats: education-and-publishing-integrity-focused, not agentic-AI-specific — most reviewed studies concern generative AI use in writing and coursework rather than autonomous agents, so the book should draw on it for the authorship/disclosure/integrity-governance argument, not for agent-specific claims; as with any review completed in December 2025, it cannot capture 2026 policy developments (such as the Elsevier update above), which the book should cite separately.
