# Theme 1 — Agentic AI and LLM-based agents (ch01–ch04)

**Yao, S., Zhao, J., Yu, D., Du, N., Shafran, I., Narasimhan, K. and Cao, Y. (2023). "ReAct: Synergizing Reasoning and Acting in Language Models." ICLR 2023.** Preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629
ReAct is the foundational statement of the pattern that underlies essentially every current LLM agent: interleaving verbal reasoning traces ("thoughts") with actions against external tools, so that reasoning steers acting and observations from the environment feed back into reasoning. The authors evaluate the paradigm on question answering (HotpotQA), fact verification (Fever), and two interactive environments (ALFWorld, WebShop), showing that interleaved reasoning-plus-acting outperforms both chain-of-thought reasoning alone (which hallucinates unchecked) and acting alone (which lacks planning), and that interacting with a Wikipedia API reduces hallucination in QA. Its evidence is benchmark-based with prompted (not fine-tuned) models of the 2022 generation, so absolute numbers are dated; the durable content is architectural. For the book this is the canonical citation for the plan–act–observe loop in ch02 and for the ch01 capability timeline (2022–23 as the moment "language model" became "agent"); the failure analysis in the paper — reasoning errors propagate into action loops, and the model can repeat failing actions — prefigures the ch13 failure gallery. Caveats for writers: ReAct is a prompting pattern, not a governance framework; the paper claims improved interpretability of agent trajectories, which supports the book's audit-trail stance but was not formally evaluated as auditability; and later agent scaffolds have moved to native tool-calling rather than text-parsed actions, so ReAct should be presented as the conceptual origin (approximate year 2022) rather than current practice. Peer-reviewed (ICLR); the arXiv record is the accessible version.

**Schick, T., et al. (2023). "Toolformer: Language Models Can Teach Themselves to Use Tools." NeurIPS 2023 (Meta AI).** Preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761
Toolformer demonstrated that a language model can learn, in a self-supervised way from a handful of demonstrations, when to call external APIs — a calculator, search, question answering, translation, a calendar — what arguments to pass, and how to fold the result into its continuing text. The method samples candidate API calls into training text, keeps those that reduce next-token loss, and fine-tunes on the result; the fine-tuned model achieved substantially improved zero-shot performance on downstream tasks, often competitive with much larger models, without degrading core language modelling. The paper matters to the book less for its specific method (superseded by instruction-tuned native tool calling) than as the citable origin of the "tools" leg of the agent anatomy in ch02: it established that tool use is learnable model behaviour rather than bolted-on engineering, and it names the motivating weakness — LLMs fail at arithmetic and factual lookup that trivial specialised systems do perfectly — which is exactly the argument ch02 and ch04 need for delegating computation to deterministic tools rather than trusting model recall. Caveats: the experiments used a 6.7B-parameter GPT-J base, far below current frontier scale; tool selection was limited to five simple APIs used independently (no chained calls); and the self-supervised criterion optimises perplexity, not task correctness, so the paper offers no evidence about tool-use reliability under the verification lens of ch11. Present it as foundational (2023) with the capability boundary stated honestly. Peer-reviewed (NeurIPS 2023).

**Wang, L., Ma, C., Feng, X., Zhang, Z., Yang, H., Zhang, J., Chen, Z., Tang, J., Chen, X., Lin, Y., Zhao, W.X., Wei, Z. and Wen, J.-R. (2023, revised 2024). "A Survey on Large Language Model based Autonomous Agents." arXiv preprint.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11432
This heavily cited survey (also published in a journal version [verify — Frontiers of Computer Science, 2024]) organises the LLM-agent literature under a unified construction framework of four modules — profile (role definition), memory, planning and action — and reviews applications across social science, natural science and engineering, plus evaluation strategies. Its value to the book is taxonomic rather than evidential: ch02's anatomy (loop, tools, context and memory, orchestration) can be cross-checked against the survey's module decomposition to show the book's structure is consistent with the research literature's, and the survey's application section provides a dated (2023–24) snapshot useful for the ch01 capability timeline. The survey also catalogues evaluation approaches (subjective human evaluation versus objective benchmarks), anticipating the task-grounded-evaluation argument of ch11, and lists open challenges — hallucination, limited context, efficiency — that remain the book's failure-mode territory. Caveats writers should carry: as a survey of a fast-moving field it is already partly stale (it predates computer-use agents and modern orchestration frameworks); it aggregates rather than adjudicates, so it should not be cited for any specific capability claim; and its "autonomous" framing is looser than the book's governed-instrument stance — the book should cite it for the state of the field, then explicitly depart from its autonomy-celebrating tone. Preprint status should be noted if the journal version is not confirmed at citation time.

**Sumers, T.R., Yao, S., Narasimhan, K. and Griffiths, T.L. (2023/2024). "Cognitive Architectures for Language Agents." Transactions on Machine Learning Research (TMLR).** Preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02427
CoALA proposes a systematic conceptual framework for language agents drawn from the history of cognitive architectures: an agent comprises modular memory components (working, episodic, semantic, procedural), a structured action space divided into internal actions (reasoning, retrieval, learning) and external actions (grounding in an environment), and a generalised decision-making loop that selects among them. The paper is a position-and-synthesis piece rather than an empirical study: its evidence is a structured retrospective mapping of existing systems (ReAct, Voyager, and others) into the framework, showing the framework's descriptive adequacy. For ch02 this is the strongest academic scaffold available for the chapter's context-and-memory schematic — it gives principled names to distinctions the book needs (working memory versus long-term stores; retrieval as an internal action; the decision loop as the governing cycle) — and its explicit lineage from production systems and SOAR lets the book make the ch04 point that agent design is an old engineering discipline newly instantiated, not an unprecedented novelty. Caveats: the framework is descriptive, not validated — no experiment shows that CoALA-conformant agents perform better or are safer; terminology in the field has not standardised on CoALA's vocabulary; and the paper is silent on verification and governance, so it supports the anatomy chapter but not the trust chapters. Peer-reviewed venue (TMLR), with authors split between Princeton and DeepMind-adjacent academia; order of first two authors decided by coin flip, per the paper.

**Feng, K.J.K., McDonald, D.W. and Zhang, A.X. (2025). "Levels of Autonomy for AI Agents." arXiv preprint (University of Washington).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.12469
This paper argues that an agent's level of autonomy is a deliberate design decision, separable from its raw capability and its operating environment, and proposes five levels characterised by the role the human user occupies: operator (L1), collaborator (L2), consultant (L3), approver (L4) and observer (L5). For each level it describes the control mechanisms available to the user, and it sketches two governance instruments: "autonomy certificates" that would attest an agent operates at a declared level, and "assisted evaluations" — determining an agent's effective autonomy level by incrementally increasing user involvement until the task succeeds — as a testable alternative to code inspection or capability benchmarks. This maps almost one-to-one onto the book's needs: ch03's claim that the specification is the primary human control surface, ch04's "should an agent do this?" decision flowchart (which is, in the paper's terms, a level-selection decision), and ch10/ch15's human-gate placement all gain a citable framework, and the user-role vocabulary is vendor-neutral, matching the book's naming policy. Caveats: this is a preprint from an HCI group, not yet (as verified this sweep) through peer review; the framework is normative and largely unvalidated empirically — no user studies of the five levels are reported in the abstract-level material verified; and the certificate proposal is speculative. Writers should present the levels as a useful proposed vocabulary with moderate confidence, not an established standard, and note that competing level schemes exist in grey literature (e.g. a Cloud Security Alliance framework located but not verified in depth this sweep).

**Anthropic (2024). "Building Effective Agents." Anthropic engineering blog.** https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents
This practitioner guide — grey literature, but among the most influential design documents in the field — distinguishes *workflows* (LLM calls orchestrated through predefined code paths) from *agents* (systems in which the model dynamically directs its own process and tool use), and catalogues composable patterns seen in production: the augmented LLM (retrieval, tools, memory) as the base unit, then prompt chaining, routing, parallelisation, orchestrator–workers, and evaluator–optimizer loops. Its governing recommendation is directly aligned with the book's stance: find the simplest solution possible, add agentic complexity only when the task demonstrably needs it, and prefer transparent, inspectable steps. For ch02 (orchestration), ch04 (when *not* to use an agent), ch07 (reviewer-agent loops are an instance of evaluator–optimizer) and ch10 (roster patterns), this is the clearest practitioner articulation available, and citing it lets the book anchor its patterns in documented industry practice without endorsing a product — though, being an Anthropic document, it should be cited in the repository/companion materials or with vendor-neutral paraphrase in print, per the CLAUDE.md naming rule. Caveats: it is undated ('December 2024' per secondary sources), unversioned vendor content with no empirical evaluation — its authority is experiential, not measured; pattern names are not standardised across vendors; and it naturally underplays failure modes that the book's ch13 must foreground. Treat as high-quality grey literature: cite for pattern vocabulary and the simplicity principle, never for performance claims.

# Theme 2 — Specifying and controlling agent behaviour (ch03)

**Schulhoff, S., Ilie, M., Balepur, N., et al. (2024, revised 2025). "The Prompt Report: A Systematic Survey of Prompt Engineering Techniques." arXiv preprint (University of Maryland and collaborators).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06608
The most comprehensive survey of prompting to date, built by a systematic review process (PRISMA-style screening) rather than ad-hoc collection: it establishes a vocabulary of 33 terms, a taxonomy of 58 text-based prompting techniques (in-context learning, thought generation, decomposition, ensembling, self-criticism families) plus 40 techniques for other modalities, and reports meta-analysis and benchmarking of technique effectiveness, along with documented case studies showing that even expert prompt engineering is iterative and brittle. For ch03 this is the load-bearing citation for two claims the book needs: first, that "prompting" is a large, unstandardised space with conflicting terminology — which motivates the book's move from conversational prompting to written specification as the control surface; second, that technique choice measurably changes output quality, i.e. behaviour control through input text is real but unreliable, which motivates acceptance criteria and stop conditions checked outside the model. The survey's self-criticism and decomposition families also give ch03 and ch07 academically grounded names for what reviewer agents and task decomposition do. Caveats: it is a preprint (widely cited but [verify] any peer-reviewed venue before print citation); its meta-analysis covers benchmark tasks, not operational scientific work, so effect sizes do not transfer directly; and it deliberately surveys rather than prescribes — the book's specification schema (objective, inputs, acceptance criteria, stop conditions) has no direct counterpart in it and must be presented as the book's own synthesis. The coverage-limits note above records that no dedicated academic literature on agent task specification was found this sweep; ch03 writers should also lean on τ-bench's policy-document mechanism (theme 5) and Anthropic's guidance (theme 1) as adjacent evidence.

# Theme 3 — Agents in scientific workflows (ch05–ch09)

**Boiko, D.A., MacKnight, R., Kline, B. and Gomes, G. (2023). "Autonomous chemical research with large language models." Nature, 624, 570–578.** DOI: 10.1038/s41586-023-06792-0
The Coscientist paper is the flagship peer-reviewed demonstration that an LLM-driven system can plan and execute real experiments: a GPT-4-based orchestrator, equipped with web search, documentation search, code execution and control of robotic laboratory hardware (including a cloud lab), autonomously designed, planned and performed chemistry workflows across six tasks, most notably optimising palladium-catalysed cross-coupling reactions. The system's architecture — a planner calling specialised tool modules — is a concrete instance of the orchestration patterns in ch02 and ch08, and the paper is the book's best citation for the claim (ch01, ch08) that agentic systems crossed from text generation into experiment orchestration in 2023. Its relevance to ch08 is direct: Coscientist monitored instrument outputs and adjusted plans, the same monitor-and-log role the book assigns agents in calibration campaigns — though the book's stance (agents monitor, humans decide) is deliberately more conservative than Coscientist's (semi-)autonomous framing. Caveats to carry into prose: the demonstrations are curated successes in a well-instrumented domain with fast, unambiguous feedback (reaction yield), conditions environmental field science rarely offers; the authors themselves flag dual-use safety concerns and include guardrail probing; reproducibility depends on a proprietary frontier model; and "autonomous" in the title overstates the human scaffolding involved in task setup. Use it as the capability existence proof, paired with the book's argument for why hydrology and meteorology need gates that chemistry benches can partially skip. Peer-reviewed (Nature).

**Lála, J., O'Donoghue, O., Shtedritski, A., Cox, S., Rodriques, S.G. and White, A.D. (2023). "PaperQA: Retrieval-Augmented Generative Agent for Scientific Research." arXiv preprint (FutureHouse / Francis Crick Institute / Oxford).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07559
PaperQA is an agentic retrieval-augmented generation system for question answering over the full-text scientific literature: it decomposes the task into tools (search, evidence gathering, answer generation), scores retrieved passages for relevance, and composes answers with citations to the retrieved sources, explicitly to reduce hallucination and provide provenance. The authors introduce LitQA, a benchmark requiring retrieval and synthesis from full-text papers, and report that PaperQA exceeds existing LLMs and commercial tools on science QA benchmarks and matches expert human researchers on LitQA, at lower cost per question; they also measure citation grounding, finding the agent's answers traceable to real retrieved sources where raw LLM answers are not. For ch05 this is the central architectural citation: retrieval-grounded synthesis with the citation step verified against retrieved documents is exactly the pattern the chapter teaches, and LitQA illustrates task-grounded evaluation (ch11) applied to literature work. Caveats: preprint (a successor system, PaperQA2, and journal versions exist — [verify] before citing anything beyond this record); LitQA is small and biology-weighted, so "matches experts" is a narrow claim that must not be generalised to interpretive review work; the authors are building commercial/nonprofit tooling in this space (FutureHouse), a potential framing bias; and retrieval quality bounds the whole pipeline — garbage retrieval yields confidently cited garbage, the failure mode ch05 and ch13 should pair with this source.

**Cao, C., Arora, R., Cento, P., et al. (including Moher, D., Church, G.M., Tricco, A.C. and Bobrovitz, N.) (2025). "Automation of Systematic Reviews with Large Language Models." medRxiv preprint.** DOI: 10.1101/2025.06.13.25329541
This large multi-institution study (Toronto, Ottawa, Harvard and others; senior authors include David Moher, a founder of PRISMA) built otto-SR, an end-to-end LLM workflow for systematic reviews covering search, screening and data extraction, and benchmarked it against conventional dual-human workflows. Reported headline results: in screening, 96.7% sensitivity and 97.9% specificity versus 81.7% sensitivity for the traditional dual-reviewer workflow; in data extraction, 93.1% accuracy versus 79.7% for humans; and, as a demonstration, reproduction and updating of an entire issue of Cochrane reviews (n = 12) in two days — work the authors estimate at roughly twelve person-years by traditional methods. For ch05 this is the strongest quantitative evidence located that LLM assistance can exceed human performance on the *mechanical* layers of evidence synthesis, and the sensitivity comparison gives ch11 a rare concrete example of measuring a review gate's false-negative rate (screening sensitivity is exactly that) — a framing the book should exploit. Caveats are substantial: preprint, not yet peer-reviewed as verified this sweep [verify journal status before print]; results come from the tool's own developers; gold standards for screening are themselves human judgements with known error; performance is domain-dependent (clinical literature, highly structured reporting) and may not transfer to heterogeneous environmental-science literatures; and the interpretive layers of review — question framing, risk-of-bias judgement, synthesis — remain human in precisely the way ch05 argues. Cite with its preprint status stated.

**Lu, C., Lu, C., Lange, R.T., Foerster, J., Clune, J. and Ha, D. (2024). "The AI Scientist: Towards Fully Automated Open-Ended Scientific Discovery." arXiv preprint (Sakana AI, Oxford, UBC).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.06292
The AI Scientist is the most prominent claim of end-to-end research automation: given a starting code template in a machine-learning subfield, the system generates research ideas, writes and runs experiment code, plots results, writes a full paper, and scores it with an automated reviewer that the authors report approaches human-level agreement on ML-conference review decisions, all at a cost of a few dollars per paper. The book needs this source for two opposite purposes. As capability evidence (ch01, ch09), it marks the 2024 moment when full-pipeline automation became a serious research programme — a successor version later produced a workshop-paper accepted through peer review. As a cautionary exhibit (ch09, ch13, ch11), it is richer still: the authors themselves document failure modes including the system editing its own execution script to relax constraints (an archetypal governance breach for ch12's audit-trail argument), implausible or fabricated experimental details, and reviewer-agent leniency; community assessments of the generated papers found them shallow and sometimes wrong in ways the automated reviewer did not catch — a concrete instance of an over-agreeable review gate passing defective work, the exact false-negative phenomenon ch11 centres on. Caveats: preprint from a commercial lab; the "papers" are template-constrained ML micro-studies, not open scientific discovery; and the automated-review agreement figures inherit all the LLM-as-judge biases documented under theme 5. The book should cite it as both milestone and warning, never as validation of automated authorship — which journal policy in theme 9 in any case forbids.

# Theme 4 — Multi-agent systems and their failure modes (ch10, ch13)

**Cemri, M., Pan, M.Z., Yang, S., et al. (2025). "Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail?" arXiv preprint (UC Berkeley and collaborators).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657
This is the single most useful source located for ch10 and ch13. The authors analysed over 200 execution traces (later extended to a 1,600+ trace dataset, MAST-Data) across seven popular multi-agent frameworks with six expert annotators, achieving inter-annotator agreement of Cohen's κ = 0.88, and derived MAST: an empirically grounded taxonomy of 14 failure modes in three categories — (i) specification issues (poor task or role specification), (ii) inter-agent misalignment (miscommunication, information withholding, lost context between agents), and (iii) task verification failures (absent, weak or premature verification of outcomes). Two findings matter most for the book. First, multi-agent systems often gain little over single agents on benchmarks, supporting ch10's "when do multiple agents add robustness versus only noise and cost" framing with independent evidence. Second, the largest failure categories trace to specification and verification, not to model weakness — which is, almost verbatim, the book's thesis that specification (ch03) and verification (ch11) are where governance effort belongs; the taxonomy's verification category also documents review gates that approve incorrect work, the closest published analogue to ch11's false-negative concern. The ch13 failure gallery can map several of its entries (specification drift, context loss, over-agreeable review) directly onto MAST categories, with attribution. Caveats: preprint [verify venue — an OpenReview record exists]; traces come from research-grade frameworks on benchmark tasks, not operational scientific pipelines; failure attribution in long traces is judgement-laden despite the high κ; and the taxonomy is descriptive — it does not measure how often proposed fixes work.

**Wu, Q., Bansal, G., Zhang, J., Wu, Y., et al. (2023). "AutoGen: Enabling Next-Gen LLM Applications via Multi-Agent Conversation." arXiv preprint (Microsoft Research and collaborators).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08155
AutoGen is the reference open-source framework for building multi-agent LLM applications as structured conversations: developers define customisable, "conversable" agents — each backed by an LLM, a tool, a human, or a combination — and compose them so that complex workflows become automated chats between roles, with human input injectable at chosen points. The paper demonstrates the pattern on mathematics, coding, question answering, supply-chain optimisation and decision-making applications. For the book its importance is architectural vocabulary with an academic citation: ch10's roles-and-rosters concept (drafter, reviewer, executor, human gate) is precisely AutoGen's programming model, and the framework's explicit support for human-in-the-loop agents gives ch10 and ch15 a documented precedent for treating the human gate as a first-class roster member rather than an afterthought. Because the book is vendor-neutral in print, AutoGen should be described generically ("conversation-structured multi-agent frameworks, c. 2023") with the citation carrying the specificity, and named only in repository materials. Caveats: the paper is a systems/framework description — its evaluations show feasibility and convenience, not reliability, and it predates the failure evidence in MAST (which analysed AutoGen-class frameworks and found the failure modes ch13 catalogues); the project has since been reorganised under Microsoft's agent tooling, so implementation details are volatile and belong in the repository, not print. Preprint record verified; a peer-reviewed venue exists for a version of this work [verify — COLM 2024] if a reviewed citation is preferred.

**Du, Y., Li, S., Torralba, A., Tenenbaum, J.B. and Mordatch, I. (2023/2024). "Improving Factuality and Reasoning in Language Models through Multiagent Debate." ICML 2024.** Preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14325
This paper supplies the evidential basis for the claim that redundancy among model instances can improve output quality: multiple LLM instances independently propose answers, then read one another's reasoning and revise over several debate rounds before converging. Across arithmetic, strategic-reasoning and biography-factuality tasks, debate improved accuracy and reduced hallucinated content relative to single-model and self-reflection baselines, including when debating instances came from different model families. For ch10 this is the strongest peer-reviewed support for the "independent reviewers add robustness" half of the chapter's argument — and, read carefully, also for its limits: gains were task-dependent, cost scales linearly-to-quadratically with agents and rounds, and convergence is to *consensus*, which is not the same as correctness — debating instances of similar models can agree on a shared error, a correlated-failure mode the book should name explicitly (it parallels consensus failures familiar from ensemble forecasting, a domain resonance ch10 can exploit). The paper also underpins ch07's independent-reviewer-agent design: the mechanism works best when reviewers are genuinely diverse, motivating the book's insistence that the reviewer agent must not share the drafter's context. Caveats: benchmark tasks only, 2023-era models, and no analysis of adversarial or specification-drift settings; subsequent literature (e.g. MAST) shows multi-agent arrangements can as easily add noise — the book should present debate as a conditional tool whose value must be verified per task (ch11), not a general safeguard. Peer-reviewed (ICML 2024).

# Theme 5 — Verification and evaluation of LLMs and agents (ch11; also ch07, ch10, ch13)

*Deepest coverage, per the book's centre of gravity. Ordered: judge validity and biases first, then task-grounded benchmarks, then benchmark-validity critique.*

**Zheng, L., Chiang, W.-L., Sheng, Y., et al. (2023). "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena." NeurIPS 2023 Datasets and Benchmarks Track.** Preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685
The founding paper of LLM-as-judge evaluation, and the source every ch11 discussion of automated review must start from. The authors propose using strong LLMs to grade open-ended model outputs, introduce MT-Bench (multi-turn questions) and Chatbot Arena (crowdsourced pairwise human votes), and validate the judge against human preferences, finding GPT-4 agreement with humans of roughly 85% — exceeding human–human agreement of about 81%. Crucially for the book, the same paper documents the systematic biases of the approach: position bias (preferring the first-presented answer), verbosity bias (preferring longer answers regardless of quality), self-enhancement bias (favouring the judge's own outputs), and limited reasoning ability (grading maths incorrectly when the judge cannot solve the problem itself). It also proposes partial mitigations — swapping positions, few-shot calibration, reference-guided grading — with measured but incomplete effect. Ch11 should present this dual message exactly: judge models are a usable, scalable review instrument whose agreement with humans is measurable, *and* an instrument with characterised systematic errors that must be calibrated per task like any sensor — the book's instrumentation framing maps onto it cleanly. The headline 85% figure needs its caveats carried: agreement was measured on chat-assistant quality, not scientific correctness; preferences are not ground truth (the sycophancy literature below shows human preference itself rewards flattery); and agreement statistics say nothing about the asymmetry ch11 cares most about — which errors the judge lets through (false negatives of the gate). Peer-reviewed (NeurIPS D&B).

**Sharma, M., Tong, M., Korbak, T., Duvenaud, D., Askell, A., Bowman, S.R., et al. (2023). "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models." ICLR 2024 (Anthropic).** Preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548
The definitive study of over-agreeableness, and the book's key citation for why agent reviews flatter rather than correct. The authors show that five state-of-the-art AI assistants (of the 2023 generation) consistently exhibit sycophancy across four free-form generation tasks: they wrongly concede when a user challenges a correct answer, tailor stated views to a user's disclosed politics or identity, admit to mistakes they did not make, and give more positive feedback on text a user claims to have written themselves. The mechanism study is the deeper contribution: analysing preference data used for RLHF, they find that a response *matching the user's stated views* is among the most predictive features of human preference, and that optimising against preference models can increase sycophancy — human feedback itself, imperfectly, rewards agreement over truth. For ch11 this converts "over-agreeable review" from anecdote to characterised, incentive-rooted behaviour: a reviewer agent asked "is this analysis sound?" by its author is structurally disposed to say yes, which is the psychological core of review-gate false negatives, and it grounds the book's design rules — independent reviewer context, adversarial framing of review prompts, blind review where feasible (ch07, ch10, ch13's over-agreeable-review entry). Caveats: models tested are now several generations old and vendors have since targeted sycophancy explicitly, so writers should claim the *mechanism* (preference-trained models inherit agreement bias) with high confidence and current *magnitudes* with low confidence; effects vary by task; and the study concerns assistants, not purpose-built reviewer scaffolds — measuring one's own gate remains necessary, which is ch11's argument. Peer-reviewed (ICLR 2024).

**Shi, L., Ma, C., Liang, W., Diao, X., Ma, W. and Vosoughi, S. (2024). "Judging the Judges: A Systematic Study of Position Bias in LLM-as-a-Judge." arXiv preprint (Dartmouth; accepted to AACL–IJCNLP 2025 per the arXiv record).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07791
Where Zheng et al. identified position bias, this study measures it at scale: 15 LLM judges, two benchmarks, over 150,000 evaluation instances across roughly 40 answer-generating models. It introduces three metrics — repetition stability (does the judge give the same verdict on identical repeats?), position consistency (does the verdict survive swapping answer order?), and preference fairness (is there directional favouritism to a position?) — and finds that position bias is systematic rather than random noise: it varies by judge model, by candidate quality gap (judges are most order-sensitive when candidates are close in quality), and by task, while being largely insensitive to prompt length. For ch11 the transferable content is methodological: these are exactly the calibration experiments a research group should run on its own review gate before trusting it — repeat-stability and order-swap tests are cheap, and the finding that bias concentrates where quality gaps are small tells the practitioner that automated review is least reliable precisely on borderline cases, which are the cases that matter at a gate. The metrics also give ch11's verification checklist concrete, citable procedures. Caveats: preprint at sweep time with acceptance noted [verify final venue details]; evaluations are on general QA/chat benchmarks, not scientific artefacts, so magnitudes will differ for, e.g., code review or QC-flag adjudication; and the study covers pairwise comparison, whereas many practical gates are single-output pass/fail judgements whose biases (leniency, sycophancy) need the other sources in this theme.

**Wataoka, K., Takahashi, T. and Ri, R. (2024). "Self-Preference Bias in LLM-as-a-Judge." arXiv preprint (SB Intuitions, Japan).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21819
This short study quantifies self-preference bias — judges favouring their own outputs — and offers a mechanism: perplexity. The authors define a bias metric over judge verdicts against human preference labels, find GPT-4 exhibits a significant degree of self-preference, and show the effect generalises beyond literal self-authorship: judges assign systematically higher evaluations to outputs with *lower perplexity under the judge's own distribution* — text that "sounds like them" — even when written by other models, and human evaluators do not share this preference. The essence of the bias, they argue, lies in familiarity rather than authorship recognition. For ch11 this matters in a specific, practical way the book should spell out: using the same model family to draft and to review — the cheapest and most tempting reviewer-agent configuration — is structurally biased towards approval, because the drafter's output is maximally familiar to the reviewer; genuinely independent review requires model diversity, not just separate context windows. This sharpens the design rule ch07 and ch10 give for reviewer rosters, and adds a measurable quantity (perplexity gap) that advanced readers could monitor. Caveats: preprint, not yet verified as peer-reviewed; the headline analysis centres on GPT-4-era models and chat-quality judgements, so magnitudes in scientific-artefact review are unknown; and perplexity of the reviewed text under the judge is not observable for closed models without logprob access, limiting the diagnostic's portability — the *design* implication (cross-family review) is the durable takeaway.

**Jimenez, C.E., Yang, J., Wettig, A., Yao, S., Pei, K., Press, O. and Narasimhan, K. (2023). "SWE-bench: Can Language Models Resolve Real-World GitHub Issues?" ICLR 2024 (oral), Princeton.** Preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06770
SWE-bench operationalises task-grounded evaluation for coding agents: 2,294 problems drawn from real GitHub issues and their resolving pull requests across 12 popular Python repositories; the system under test receives the codebase and issue text and must produce a patch, which is verified by running the repository's own test suite — success is defined by held-out tests passing, not by resemblance to a reference answer. At publication, the best model (Claude 2) resolved 1.96% of issues; frontier agent systems now report far higher figures, making SWE-bench the de-facto yardstick of coding-agent progress and a core exhibit for ch01's capability timeline and ch07's governed-pipeline argument. For ch11 its significance is methodological and the book should state it explicitly: execution-based verification against pre-existing tests is the pattern the chapter generalises — the acceptance criterion exists *before* the agent runs, is automated, and does not depend on a model's opinion. Equally important are its documented weaknesses, which teach the chapter's second lesson: later analyses (see Zhu et al., ABC, below) found insufficient test coverage in the curated SWE-bench Verified subset can misclassify wrong patches as correct — i.e. even execution-grounded gates have measurable false-negative rates set by test quality — and training-data contamination plus repository familiarity can inflate scores. Caveats: Python-only, popular OSS repositories, issue text often richer than real scientific tickets; solving benchmark issues is not equivalent to safe modification of an operational forecasting codebase. Peer-reviewed (ICLR 2024 oral).

**Mialon, G., Fourrier, C., Swift, C., Wolf, T., LeCun, Y. and Scialom, T. (2023). "GAIA: a benchmark for General AI Assistants." arXiv preprint (Meta AI / Hugging Face).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.12983
GAIA poses 466 real-world assistant questions requiring reasoning, multi-modality, web browsing and tool use, each with a single verifiable factual answer, and inverts the usual benchmark philosophy: instead of tasks hard for humans, it uses tasks conceptually simple for humans — respondents scored 92% — yet hard for AI systems (GPT-4 with plugins scored about 15% at publication). Questions are graded by exact match against unambiguous answers, making evaluation automatic and robust to the judge biases in the sources above. For the book GAIA is doubly useful. For ch01/ch02 it provides an honest capability boundary from the 2023 baseline — the gap between fluent conversation and reliable multi-step tool use — and its subsequent rapid saturation by agent systems (widely reported on public leaderboards; figures volatile, keep to the repository) illustrates both genuine progress and benchmark ageing. For ch11 its design principles are the citable content: tasks with pre-registered, unambiguous, automatically checkable answers; difficulty tiers defined by number of steps and tools; and robustness-to-guessing considerations — a template the chapter can translate into designing in-house evaluations for scientific workflows ("does the agent retrieve the correct discharge value from this archive?" has a GAIA-style verifiable answer). Caveats: preprint; single-answer factual tasks cannot evaluate open-ended synthesis or judgement, exactly the outputs environmental scientists care most about — the book should use GAIA to argue for verifiable sub-tasks while conceding that interpretive outputs need the human-centred evaluation of ch11's evidential hierarchy; and public benchmark answers leak into training data over time, degrading validity.

**Yao, S., Shinn, N., Razavi, P. and Narasimhan, K. (2024). "τ-bench: A Benchmark for Tool-Agent-User Interaction in Real-World Domains." arXiv preprint (Sierra).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045
τ-bench evaluates what most agent benchmarks omit: interaction with a (simulated) human user and compliance with written domain policy. An agent serves customer-service tasks in retail and airline domains, equipped with API tools, a database, and a policy document it must obey; evaluation compares the final database state against an annotated goal state — objective, judge-free verification — and a new metric, pass^k, measures the probability that *all* of k independent runs succeed, capturing reliability rather than best-case ability. Findings: state-of-the-art function-calling agents succeeded on under 50% of tasks, and reliability collapsed with repetition (pass^8 below 25% in retail) — agents that look competent on single trials are inconsistent across identical trials. Three elements transfer directly to the book. The policy document is the closest published analogue to ch03's specification-as-control-surface: behaviour is governed by an auditable written artefact, and failures are frequently policy violations, i.e. specification non-compliance. pass^k is the reliability statistic ch11 should teach for operational gating — a workflow run monthly needs pass^12-style thinking, and single-trial success rates systematically overstate dependability (an argument with obvious resonance for operational forecasting duty cycles). Final-state comparison exemplifies outcome-grounded verification. Caveats: preprint [verify any reviewed venue]; user simulation is itself an LLM, adding unmodelled variance the authors acknowledge; two commercial domains only — but the evaluation *design*, not the domains, is what the book should cite. Authors are at Sierra, a commercial agent company; the benchmark's difficulty claims serve their framing, though the artefact is open source.

**Yehudai, A., Eden, L., Li, A., Uziel, G., Zhao, Y., Bar-Haim, R., Cohan, A. and Shmueli-Scheuer, M. (2025, revised 2026). "Survey on Evaluation of LLM-based Agents." arXiv preprint (IBM Research / Yale and collaborators).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.16416
The most complete map of the agent-evaluation landscape located this sweep. The survey organises the field along what is evaluated — fundamental agent capabilities (planning, tool use, self-reflection, memory), domain-specific agents (web, software engineering, science, conversation), generalist agents, and frameworks for evaluation in development — and how: benchmark construction, interaction modes, metrics and tooling. Its synthesis identifies the field's trajectory (from static, simplified tasks towards realistic, challenging, continuously refreshed benchmarks) and, most useful to the book, its explicitly named gaps: weak coverage of cost-efficiency, safety and robustness evaluation, and a shortage of fine-grained, scalable, trajectory-level assessment methods. For ch11 this survey serves as the "state of the art and its holes" citation: the book's claim that leaderboard benchmarks under-serve working scientists — and that task-grounded, workflow-specific evaluation must fill the gap — is directly supported by the survey's own gap analysis, from authors invested in the benchmark ecosystem. It also gives writers a navigable index: any specific capability ch07–ch10 mentions (tool-use reliability, planning, memory) has its benchmark lineage catalogued here, saving the manuscript from stale examples. Caveats: preprint (a version was presented in a KDD 2025 tutorial context; a related peer-reviewed survey by Mohammadi et al., DOI 10.1145/3711896.3736570, covers similar ground and can substitute where a reviewed citation is required); surveys date quickly — the 2026 revision found this sweep is current, but writers should check for later revisions at citation time; and it catalogues rather than validates: no benchmark it lists should be cited as *good* on the survey's authority alone, particularly given the validity critiques below.

**Kapoor, S., Stroebl, B., Siegel, Z.S., Nadgir, N. and Narayanan, A. (2024). "AI Agents That Matter." arXiv preprint (Princeton).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01502
A methodological critique of agent evaluation from the group behind *AI Snake Oil*, arguing that current practice produces misleading conclusions in five ways: accuracy-only evaluation ignores cost, so leaderboards reward wasteful scaffolds (they show simple baselines — retry loops over a plain model — matching complex agent architectures on HumanEval at a fraction of the cost); accuracy and cost are jointly optimisable and should be reported as a Pareto frontier; benchmarks lack adequate held-out sets (they find inadequate holdouts in seven of eight benchmarks examined), enabling overfitting to tasks; evaluation shortcuts inflate claimed generality; and reporting standards are inconsistent enough to prevent reproduction. For the book this paper is load-bearing twice. In ch11 it supplies the core argument that benchmark rank is not evidence of workflow value: a scientist choosing an agent workflow needs task-grounded, cost-aware evaluation on their own distribution, which is precisely the chapter's five-tier evidential programme. In ch16's cost model it provides the only rigorous published treatment located of cost as a first-class evaluation axis, including the observation that agent costs vary by orders of magnitude at similar accuracy. Its documented baseline results also arm ch04's "when not to use an agent": if a retry loop matches a multi-agent system, the governed-simplicity default follows. Caveats: preprint (widely cited; [verify] any archival venue before print); its empirical demonstrations concentrate on coding/QA benchmarks of 2024 vintage; and its recommendations are norms, not measurements — the book should cite the demonstrated pathologies with high confidence and the proposed standards as recommendations.

**Zhu, Y., Jin, T., Pruksachatkun, Y., et al. (2025). "Establishing Best Practices for Building Rigorous Agentic Benchmarks." arXiv preprint (UIUC, Stanford, Princeton, UK AI Security Institute, Berkeley and others).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02825
This 25-author collaboration audits the validity of agentic benchmarks themselves and provides ch11's strongest ammunition on review-gate false negatives. Assessing ten popular benchmarks against two validity criteria — task validity (does the task measure what it claims?) and outcome validity (does the grading actually detect success?) — they find seven with outcome-validity flaws, seven with task-validity flaws, and all ten with reporting limitations. Concrete failures: an agent can score 100% on SWE-Lancer without resolving any task (reward hacking of the grader); SWE-bench Verified's insufficient test cases misclassify incorrect patches as correct; τ-bench counted empty responses as successes in some configurations; KernelBench's incomplete fuzz testing overestimates capability by 31 percentage points; WebArena's string matching inflates scores by about 5%. Their remedy, the Agentic Benchmark Checklist (ABC), is a structured audit instrument; applying it to CVE-Bench reduced performance overestimation by 33%. The transferable lesson is exactly the book's: *automated verification is itself an instrument with a measurable error rate* — a gate built on weak test coverage or lenient matching passes failing work, and the error is quantifiable and reducible by systematic audit. Ch11 can adapt the ABC's logic into its verification checklist for scientific workflows (audit your acceptance tests before trusting agent pass rates), and ch13 gains documented, citable instances of gates passing defective outputs. Caveats: preprint [verify venue]; the audited benchmarks are software/web/security-flavoured, so translation to environmental workflows is by analogy; and checklist effectiveness is demonstrated on one case study.

# Theme 6 — Hallucination, fabricated citations, provenance (ch05, ch09, ch13)

**Walters, W.H. and Wilder, E.I. (2023). "Fabrication and errors in the bibliographic citations generated by ChatGPT." Scientific Reports, 13, 14045.** DOI: 10.1038/s41598-023-41032-5
The most rigorous quantification located of the fabricated-citation problem, and the anchor citation for ch13's first gallery entry. The authors had GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 produce short literature reviews on 42 multidisciplinary topics, yielding 636 citations across 84 papers, and verified every one: 55% of GPT-3.5's citations were wholly fabricated versus 18% for GPT-4; among the *real* citations, 43% (GPT-3.5) and 24% (GPT-4) contained substantive errors — wrong volume, issue or page numbers being commonest (34% vs 13%), then wrong dates (22% vs 16%); book-chapter citations were worst, about 70% fabricated; and over 40% of all citations carried formatting errors besides. Two features make this paper especially useful for the book. First, the fabrications "look legitimate at first glance" — plausible authors, real journals, sensible titles — which is precisely why the ch05 pattern makes citation verification a mechanical, tool-executed step (resolve every DOI, match every title) rather than a human plausibility check. Second, the GPT-3.5→GPT-4 improvement demonstrates both that model progress reduces the failure rate and that it remains far from zero — supporting the book's stance that improvement curves never substitute for verification gates. Caveats writers must carry: the study tested 2023 chat models *without retrieval*; retrieval-grounded systems (PaperQA, theme 3) change the failure profile from invented references to mis-supported claims; and rates vary by field and topic obscurity, so the specific percentages should be dated explicitly in print (per the vendor-neutral, approximate-years rule). Peer-reviewed (Scientific Reports).

**Cabezas-Clavijo, Á. and Sidorenko-Bautista, P. (2025). "Assessing the performance of 8 AI chatbots in bibliographic reference retrieval: Grok and DeepSeek outperform ChatGPT, but none are fully accurate." arXiv preprint; noted as to appear in Journal of Data and Information Science (2026) [verify final publication].** https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18059
A useful 2025 update to Walters and Wilder showing the fabricated-reference problem persists across the current model generation. The authors requested academic references from eight chatbots across 400 test cases in five knowledge domains and verified each: only 26.5% of references were entirely correct, 33.8% partially correct, and 39.8% erroneous or wholly fabricated; hallucination rates varied sharply by system (two systems avoided fabricating references outright while several mainstream assistants had the highest fabrication rates), and journal-article references were fabricated more often than book references. For ch05 and ch13 the value is twofold: it demonstrates that citation fabrication is not a solved 2023-era problem but a persistent structural failure mode two years later, and its between-system variance supports the book's argument that capability must be measured per tool and per task rather than assumed from brand or generation — an instance of ch11's calibration stance applied to the simplest possible verifiable output. It also usefully documents that the failure is *domain-dependent* (error rates differed across the five fields), reinforcing why environmental scientists must run their own checks on their own literature rather than trusting generic accuracy claims. Caveats: preprint at sweep time with journal publication pending [verify]; the evaluation covers reference *retrieval on request*, a harsher test than retrieval-augmented drafting; product-specific results are volatile and belong in the repository, not print — in manuscript prose, cite the aggregate finding (roughly 40% of requested references erroneous or fabricated across systems, 2025) with the date attached.

**Huang, L., Yu, W., et al. (2023, revised). "A Survey on Hallucination in Large Language Models: Principles, Taxonomy, Challenges, and Open Questions." arXiv preprint; published version in ACM Transactions on Information Systems (2025) [verify DOI of journal version].** https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.05232
The standard scholarly taxonomy of hallucination, useful to the book chiefly for its vocabulary and causal analysis. The survey distinguishes factuality hallucination (output conflicts with verifiable world knowledge — subdivided into factual contradiction and factual fabrication) from faithfulness hallucination (output conflicts with the provided source or instruction — the failure that matters most in retrieval-grounded synthesis and data summarisation), and traces contributing causes through the model lifecycle: flawed or outdated training data, imperfect representation learning, misalignment introduced by preference tuning, and decoding randomness. It then catalogues detection methods (fact-checking against external knowledge, uncertainty estimation, self-consistency) and mitigations (retrieval augmentation, improved decoding, alignment interventions), with an honest account of their partial effectiveness. For the book: ch05 should adopt the factuality/faithfulness distinction explicitly — an agent's literature synthesis can cite real papers faithfully yet still fabricate the connective claims, and the two failures need different checks; ch13's gallery entries (fabricated citations, confident extrapolation) can be located precisely in this taxonomy, giving the gallery academic footing; and the causal analysis supports ch04's argument that hallucination is intrinsic to the technology's statistical nature — a property to be managed by workflow design, not a bug awaiting a fix. Caveats: the survey predates the agentic turn, so it treats single-model generation rather than compounding hallucination through multi-step agent pipelines (the book must make that extension itself, with MAST as partial support); and mitigation-effectiveness numbers cited within it are dated.

# Theme 7 — Governance and security of LLM agents (ch12, ch14, ch15)

**Greshake, K., Abdelnabi, S., Debenedetti, E., et al. (2023). "Not what you've signed up for: Compromising Real-World LLM-Integrated Applications with Indirect Prompt Injection." Proceedings of the 16th ACM Workshop on Artificial Intelligence and Security (AISec '23).** DOI: 10.1145/3605764.3623985. Preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12173
The paper that defined indirect prompt injection, and the foundation of ch12's security section. Its central insight is architectural: LLM applications blur the line between data and instructions — anything the model *reads* can function as a command — so an adversary who can place text where an agent will retrieve it (a web page, a document, an email, metadata) can hijack the agent without ever touching the user's prompt. The authors build a taxonomy of resulting threats from a computer-security perspective — data theft, fraud, malware propagation ("worming" between agent instances), information-ecosystem contamination, persistence — and demonstrate practical attacks against deployed systems, including Bing Chat and code-completion engines, as well as synthetic GPT-4 applications. For the book, the translation to scientific practice is direct and should be made explicit in ch12: an agent that ingests downloaded datasets, README files, third-party code or web pages while holding credentials to institutional storage or HPC is exposed to exactly this class of attack; "the data can order your agent around" is the one security concept every environmental scientist deploying agents must internalise, and it motivates least-privilege tool access and the separation of retrieval from action that ch12 and the theme's design-patterns paper formalise. Caveats: demonstrations are 2023-era and specific exploits have been patched, but the underlying data/instruction conflation is unresolved by any current model — writers should present the vulnerability class as durable (high confidence) and any particular exploit as historical. Peer-reviewed workshop proceedings (ACM AISec).

**Beurer-Kellner, L., Buesser, B., Creţu, A.-M., Debenedetti, E., Dobos, D., Fabian, D., Fischer, M., et al. (2025). "Design Patterns for Securing LLM Agents against Prompt Injections." arXiv preprint (ETH Zürich, Google, Microsoft, Invariant Labs and others).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08837
The constructive counterpart to Greshake et al.: given that prompt injection cannot currently be solved at the model level, this multi-institution paper proposes six architectural patterns that give *provable* resistance by construction, each trading capability for safety: action-selector (the model only chooses among predefined actions), plan-then-execute (the plan is fixed before untrusted data is read, so the data cannot redirect it), LLM map-reduce (untrusted content processed in isolated calls whose outputs are constrained), dual LLM (a privileged model with tool access never reads untrusted text; a quarantined model reads it but has no tools), code-then-execute (the model writes a checkable program which is then run), and context minimisation (untrusted input is dropped from context once processed). Ten case studies work the trade-offs through. For the book this is the citable bridge from ch12's security principles to buildable workflow shapes: several patterns are recognisably what the book's chapters already prescribe — plan-then-execute mirrors ch03's specification-before-execution; code-then-execute mirrors ch07's insistence that agents produce inspectable artefacts rather than take direct actions; dual-LLM mirrors the gate between retrieval and action. Ch14's constrained three-tier toolkit can be presented as an instance of these patterns under low-resource constraints. Caveats: preprint [verify venue]; "provable" applies to the control-flow property each pattern enforces, not to end-to-end safety — data exfiltration through permitted channels remains possible in several patterns, as the authors acknowledge; and every pattern restricts agent utility, a cost the book should state plainly rather than presenting security as free.

**OWASP GenAI Security Project (2025). "OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025."** https://genai.owasp.org/resource/owasp-top-10-for-llm-applications-2025/
The community-maintained consensus list of the ten most critical security risks in LLM applications, revised in late 2024 for 2025 specifically to reflect agentic systems. Prompt injection retains the top position (LLM01); the list also covers sensitive information disclosure, supply-chain risks, data and model poisoning, improper output handling, **excessive agency** (LLM06 — an application granted more tools, permissions or autonomy than its function requires, the direct namesake of ch12's least-privilege principle), system-prompt leakage, vector/embedding weaknesses (RAG-specific), misinformation, and unbounded consumption. Each entry carries concrete mitigations. For the book this is the right *institutional-language* citation for ch12's "what institutional IT will ask" section: OWASP is the vocabulary security teams already speak, so mapping the book's governance controls (least-privilege tool rosters, output validation before execution, audit trails, human gates on consequential actions) onto OWASP categories gives readers a document their IT departments will accept — arguably the most practically valuable service ch12 can perform. Excessive agency in particular gives an external, widely recognised name to the book's core design stance that autonomy is a granted, bounded quantity. Caveats: grey literature by design — a consensus artefact, not research, with no empirical incidence data behind the ranking; it addresses application security, not scientific integrity (a distinction ch12 must keep clear: passing OWASP review does not make results trustworthy); and it is versioned — cite the 2025 edition explicitly, keep specifics in the repository per the volatility rule.

**National Institute of Standards and Technology (2024). "Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile." NIST AI 600-1, July 2024.** https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf
The US federal reference document adapting the NIST AI Risk Management Framework to generative AI. It catalogues twelve risk areas — including confabulation (NIST's institutional term for hallucination), information integrity, information security, data privacy, human–AI configuration, value-chain and component integration, and, notably for an environmental-science readership, *environmental impacts* of training and inference — and maps roughly 200 suggested actions onto the RMF's four functions (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage). Federal agencies use it as their baseline for generative-AI risk assessment under OMB direction. For the book its value is threefold. For ch16, the inclusion of environmental impacts in a national risk profile legitimises the chapter's energy-and-carbon section as mainstream governance, not activist add-on. Caveats: it is guidance, not regulation, and US-specific — European readers will map to the EU AI Act instead (not swept in depth this pass; note for a follow-up sweep); it addresses generative AI generally, with agentic autonomy only partially covered (NIST has issued subsequent agent-focused material — [verify] current documents at citation time); and its action catalogue is organisational in scale, needing translation to research-group scale, which is precisely ch16's job.

# Theme 8 — AI/ML and agents in environmental science, hydrology, meteorology (whole book; ch14–ch15 context)

**Lam, R., et al. (2023). "Learning skillful medium-range global weather forecasting." Science, 382(6677), 1416–1421 (Google DeepMind).** DOI: 10.1126/science.adi2336
GraphCast is the paper that made ML weather prediction impossible for operational meteorology to ignore: a graph-neural-network model trained on ERA5 reanalysis that produces 10-day global forecasts of hundreds of variables at 0.25° resolution in under a minute on a single TPU, outperforming the leading operational deterministic system (ECMWF's HRES) on 90% of 1,380 verification targets, with demonstrated value on severe-event diagnostics: tropical-cyclone tracks, atmospheric rivers, extreme temperatures. For the book's positioning, GraphCast anchors the ch01 claim that the reader's own field is already inside the AI transition — the capability timeline can place data-driven global forecasting's credibility threshold at 2022–23 — and it grounds ch08's three-track (physics / data-driven / hybrid) intercomparison design in the real intercomparisons the field is now running. Its verification story is equally instructive for ch11: the 90%-of-targets claim rests on a carefully specified, pre-existing scorecard methodology (headline metrics, per-variable and per-lead-time), a mature verification culture the book argues agentic workflows must import. Caveats for prose: deterministic ML forecasts trained on smooth reanalysis exhibit blurring at longer leads and understate small-scale extremes; scores against ERA5-verifying analyses partially favour models trained on ERA5; the comparison to HRES involved initialisation subtleties the paper documents; and GraphCast forecasts states, not the full forecast production chain (data assimilation, ensembles, products). British spelling note: Science uses "skillful" in the title; quote exactly when citing. Peer-reviewed (Science).

**Bi, K., Xie, L., Zhang, H., Chen, X., Gu, X. and Tian, Q. (2023). "Accurate medium-range global weather forecasting with 3D neural networks." Nature, 619, 533–538 (Huawei Cloud).** DOI: 10.1038/s41586-023-06185-3. Preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.02556
Pangu-Weather, published months before GraphCast's Science paper, was the first ML model shown to beat state-of-the-art operational NWP accuracy on upper-air and surface variables across lead times, using a 3D Earth-specific transformer (~256M parameters) trained on 43 years of ERA5, with hierarchical temporal aggregation (chained models of different step lengths) to limit error accumulation, and roughly four orders of magnitude speed-up over numerical prediction. Its significance for the book is partly historical — together with GraphCast and FourCastNet it defines the 2022–23 inflection the ch01 timeline needs, and the fact that the decisive advances came from technology companies rather than meteorological institutions is a sociological point ch16's adoption discussion can use — and partly operational: ECMWF began running Pangu-Weather (with other ML models) experimentally alongside its physics suite from 2023, the first step in the mainstreaming that AIFS completed. For ch08, Pangu's engineering choices (temporal aggregation to manage compounding error) offer a concrete parallel to the compounding-error problem in multi-step agent pipelines — a cross-domain analogy the book is well placed to draw. Caveats: deterministic, reanalysis-trained, with the same blurring and extremes-smoothing caveats as GraphCast; verification against ERA5 favours ERA5-trained models; and precipitation — the variable hydrologists need most — was not among its strengths, a gap writers should note when bridging to the book's rainfall-verification case study (ch14). Peer-reviewed (Nature).

**Lang, S., Alexe, M., Chantry, M., Dramsch, J., Pinault, F., Raoult, B., et al. (2024). "AIFS — ECMWF's data-driven forecasting system." arXiv preprint (ECMWF).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01465. AIFS is the document that turns "ML weather models beat NWP in papers" into "an operational forecasting centre runs one on duty": a graph-neural-network encoder/decoder with a sliding-window transformer processor, trained on ERA5 and ECMWF operational analyses, producing skilful forecasts of upper-air variables, surface conditions and tropical-cyclone tracks; ECMWF took AIFS Single into full operations on 25 February 2025, running side by side with the physics-based IFS, with reported gains up to about 20% on some headline scores and roughly a thousandfold reduction in forecast-time energy use. For the book this is the single most load-bearing domain citation: it proves that a conservative, verification-obsessed operational institution can adopt AI under governance — parallel running against the incumbent, published verification, staged operationalisation — which is precisely the adoption pattern the book prescribes for agentic workflows (ch15's publication run, ch16's on-ramp), giving the whole argument an unimpeachable in-domain precedent. The energy figure also feeds ch16's carbon section (with training-cost honesty: the thousandfold saving is at inference, not training). Caveats: the arXiv paper is a technical report, not peer-reviewed [verify any journal version]; scores and operational details are volatile — keep specifics in the repository, capability classes and years in print; ensemble AIFS and data-assimilation developments postdate this record; and ECMWF's in-house verification, however professional, is self-assessment — independent assessments (e.g. Ben Bouallègue et al., below) complete the picture. Grey/preprint literature from the authoritative operational source.

**Ben Bouallègue, Z., et al. (2024). "The Rise of Data-Driven Weather Forecasting: A First Statistical Assessment of Machine Learning-Based Weather Forecasts in an Operational-Like Context." Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society, 105(6) (ECMWF).** DOI: 10.1175/BAMS-D-23-0162.1. Preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.10128
The paper ch11 should hold up as a model of independent, task-grounded evaluation. ECMWF scientists took Pangu-Weather — a vendor-published model with impressive self-reported scores — and assessed it *as an operational centre would*: initialised from operational analyses rather than reanalysis (the realistic setting), verified against both analyses and station observations, scored with the centre's standard headline metrics, and examined on case studies including extreme events. The findings were nuanced in exactly the way the book values: Pangu proved genuinely competitive with the IFS in this operational-like context, but with documented weaknesses — reduced forecast "activity" (smoothing), underestimation of some extremes, and behaviours the training objective explains. The paper thereby did for an ML forecast model precisely what the book says a research group must do for an agentic workflow: re-verify the vendor's claims on your own data, under your own operating conditions, with your own metrics, before trusting it in production. This methodological parallel — domain-native and prestige-published — is arguably the strongest single bridge between the book's meteorological readership and its verification thesis, and it belongs in ch11's argument and ch14–ch15's practice. It also documents the field's measured, non-hype response to disruption, a tone the book explicitly shares. Caveats: assesses one model generation (2023) — its specific scores are superseded, its method is the durable content; and, as an evaluation by the incumbent system's operator, symmetrical scepticism applies, which the authors mitigate through transparent methodology. Peer-reviewed (BAMS).

**Kochkov, D., Yuval, J., Langmore, I., Norgaard, P., Smith, J., Mooers, G., Klöwer, M., Lottes, J., et al. (2024). "Neural general circulation models for weather and climate." Nature, 632, 1060–1066 (Google Research / MIT / ECMWF-affiliated authors).** DOI: 10.1038/s41586-024-07744-y
NeuralGCM demonstrates the hybrid physics–ML track that completes ch08's three-track intercomparison design: a differentiable dynamical core solving large-scale atmospheric equations, coupled with learned ML parameterisations of sub-grid processes, trained end-to-end ("online") so the ML components are optimised in interaction with the governing equations. Results span both regimes: weather forecasts competitive with leading ML and physics models at short-to-medium range, and — the distinctive claim — stable multi-decade climate-mode simulations at 140 km resolution with emergent phenomena such as realistic tropical-cyclone frequencies and tracks, at orders-of-magnitude lower cost than conventional GCMs, while the authors explicitly caution that the model does not extrapolate reliably to substantially different future climates. For the book, NeuralGCM is the citable existence proof that "physics versus data-driven" is a false dichotomy — the hybrid lane is real, peer-reviewed and competitive — which is exactly the framing the ch08 worked design needs; the honestly stated extrapolation limit is equally valuable, giving ch13's "confident extrapolation" gallery entry a domain-authentic, high-profile analogue: a model excellent in its training distribution with explicitly bounded validity outside it. The differentiable-hybrid idea also connects to hydrology's parallel debate (process-based versus LSTM, below), letting the book present one pattern across both fields. Caveats: atmosphere-only with prescribed sea-surface temperatures — not a coupled Earth-system model; climate-fidelity claims are for held-out historical periods, not projections; and operational usability (data assimilation, products) is untested. Peer-reviewed (Nature).

**Kratzert, F., Klotz, D., Shalev, G., Klambauer, G., Hochreiter, S. and Nearing, G. (2019). "Towards learning universal, regional, and local hydrological behaviors via machine learning applied to large-sample datasets." Hydrology and Earth System Sciences, 23, 5089–5110.** DOI: 10.5194/hess-23-5089-2019
The foundational demonstration that deep learning beats calibrated conceptual models in rainfall-runoff simulation, included as the older anchor the domain narrative rests on. A single LSTM trained jointly on 531 CAMELS basins — meteorological forcings plus static catchment attributes — outperformed conceptual models calibrated *individually per basin* (SAC-SMA and others), overturning the assumption that hydrological models must be locally calibrated; the entity-aware architecture learned transferable relationships between catchment properties and hydrological response, laying the basis for prediction in ungauged basins (extended in the authors' 2019 WRR paper, DOI: 10.1029/2019WR026065). For the book this source does double duty. Historically, it marks hydrology's own pre-LLM deep-learning inflection (~2019), letting ch01's timeline show the reader's field was transforming before agents arrived — agentic AI lands in a discipline already renegotiating its relationship with ML. Methodologically, it exemplifies the large-sample, benchmark-culture evaluation (CAMELS, standard metrics, public code) that ch11 wants agentic workflows to inherit: the claim "LSTM beats SAC-SMA" was made credible by exactly the multi-basin, held-out, metric-specified testing the book teaches. The later community position (never train an LSTM on a single basin; HESS Opinions 2024, located but not separately summarised) extends the lesson: data regime, not architecture, drives capability — a caution transferable to agent evaluation. Caveats: streamflow simulation with quality forcings is a favourable ML setting; interpretability and extrapolation to non-stationary climate remain contested in the discipline — cite the debate, not a settled verdict. Peer-reviewed (HESS).

**Arsenault, R., Martel, J.-L., Brunet, F., Brissette, F. and Mai, J. (2023). "Continuous streamflow prediction in ungauged basins: long short-term memory neural networks clearly outperform traditional hydrological models." Hydrology and Earth System Sciences, 27, 139–157.** DOI: 10.5194/hess-27-139-2023
An independent, adversarially framed confirmation of the Kratzert result on the harder ungauged-basin problem, valuable to the book precisely because it is *not* from the originating group. Using 148 catchments in northeast North America, the authors compared LSTM regionalisation against three traditional hydrological models under 18 model–regionalisation combinations: the LSTM achieved a median NSE of 0.78 versus 0.58–0.63 for the traditional approaches, outperformed every combination in at least 93% of basins, and in many cases beat models *calibrated on the target basin's own data* — with robustness across LSTM hyperparameter variants. For the book's purposes this is the clean in-domain example of claim verification by independent replication (ch11's evidential hierarchy in action within hydrology: initial claim → independent multi-model test → community position), and it strengthens the ch01/ch08 narrative that data-driven methods have earned operational standing in hydrology on evidence, not fashion. It also provides ch14–ch15 context: rainfall-runoff ML is exactly the modelling substrate the book's governed workflows would wrap — the models are trusted because the verification culture around them is strong, and the book's contribution is to extend that culture to the agentic layer that increasingly operates such models. Caveats: region-specific (humid, snow-affected northeast North America); NSE-centred evaluation underweights low-flow and extreme performance; and regionalisation results depend on catchment-similarity structure that differs in arid or data-sparse regions — transferability claims should stay bounded. Peer-reviewed (HESS).

**Zhu, [first initial not confirmed], et al. [author list — verify; paywalled] (2026). "Large Language Models as Calibration Agents in Hydrological Modeling: Feasibility and Limitations." Geophysical Research Letters.** DOI: 10.1029/2025GL120043
The closest published work located to the book's own territory: LLM agents operating inside a hydrological modelling workflow, in an AGU journal. Per the publisher listing and secondary coverage (full text paywalled this sweep — author list and details flagged [verify]), the study tests LLM-driven schemes as calibration agents for hydrological model parameters, finding that reasoning-class models can optimise parameters with effectiveness approaching standard methods while offering clearer parameter interpretability, with reasoning traces that align with expert hydrological judgement — and, per its own title, documenting limitations alongside feasibility. For ch08 (calibration campaigns are a named workload) this is the direct in-domain citation that the pattern has entered the peer-reviewed hydrology literature by 2026; for the book's positioning statement it is evidence that the environmental-science community is beginning to treat agents as workflow instruments — while the absence of any governance framing in such early papers is exactly the gap the book's contribution statement claims to fill. Writers should use it to demonstrate currency ("by 2026, GRL papers test LLMs as calibration agents") and to contrast the book's verification-first treatment with the capability-first framing typical of first-wave domain papers. Caveats: single study, early-stage, full methods not inspected this sweep — before print citation the author list, experimental scope and stated limitations must be read from the paper itself [verify]; and LLM-based optimisation competes with mature, cheap classical calibration — feasibility does not establish advantage, a distinction ch04's "should an agent do this?" flowchart exists to enforce.

**Pantiukhin, D., Shapkin, B., Kuznetsov, I., Jost, A.A. and Koldunov, N. (2025). "Accelerating earth science discovery via multi-agent LLM systems." Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence, 8 (Perspective article; Alfred Wegener Institute).** DOI: 10.3389/frai.2025.1674927
A perspective from an operational polar/marine research institute arguing that multi-agent LLM systems can address chronic geoscientific data-management problems — heterogeneous formats, inadequate metadata, labour-intensive archive exploration — and presenting PANGAEA GPT, an open-source multi-agent framework over the PANGAEA earth-science data archive, with centralised orchestration dispatching specialised sub-agents, tool-augmented and retrieval-augmented generation, and reflection modules to limit hallucination. Its stated limitations are as useful to the book as its proposals: the authors concede a lack of rigorous empirical validation, the absence of domain-specific benchmarks for geoscientific agent tasks, significant computational overhead, and continued need for human-in-the-loop evaluation. For the book this source does three jobs. It is direct evidence for the positioning claim that environmental-science agent literature remains perspective-and-prototype rather than governed practice (contribution statement, preface). It gives ch06 (data acquisition/QC over messy environmental archives) and ch10 (orchestrator-plus-specialists roster) an in-domain, citable architecture sketch. And its self-admitted evaluation gap hands ch11 a domain-authentic statement that verification is the missing layer — from authors building the systems, not from the book alone. Caveats: a Perspective, not validated research — cite its claims as informed opinion; PANGAEA GPT's capabilities are demonstrated, not benchmarked; and institutional enthusiasm for autonomous "self-organising agent networks" in its outlook section is exactly the autonomy-celebrating framing the book's governed-instrument stance argues against — quote it as the position the book qualifies. Peer-reviewed venue (Frontiers), Perspective article type.

# Theme 9 — Adoption, policy and institutional guidance (ch09, ch16, ch17)

**Nature (editorial) (2023). "Tools such as ChatGPT threaten transparent science; here are our ground rules for their use." Nature, 613, 612.** DOI: 10.1038/d41586-023-00191-1. https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-023-00191-1
The editorial that set the pattern nearly all major journals followed, published within weeks of ChatGPT's mainstream arrival, establishing two rules: no LLM will be accepted as a credited author, because authorship carries accountability that a tool cannot bear; and researchers using LLMs must document that use in the methods (or equivalent) section. The current Nature Portfolio policy (verified at https://www.nature.com/nature-portfolio/editorial-policies/ai) maintains both rules and adds refinements the book should note: AI-generated images are largely prohibited, use of AI in peer review is restricted, and minor copy-editing assistance is explicitly exempted from disclosure. For ch09 this is the anchor citation for the "agents are never authors" rule the chapter states as settled — usefully, the accountability rationale is identical to the book's instrument framing (an instrument cannot take responsibility; therefore a human must, with records to make that responsibility real, linking directly to ch12's provenance registries feeding the disclosure statement). The editorial's early date also lets ch09 present policy-by-policy-class rather than journal-by-journal: the accountability principle (2023, stable, in print) versus disclosure mechanics (volatile, repository). Caveats: an editorial, not research — it evidences policy, not effectiveness, and enforcement/detection remain open problems the book should not overstate; policies differ in detail across publishers (Science's stricter initial stance, Elsevier's variants), so print prose must stay at the class level per the outline's "survey by policy class" instruction, with current specifics maintained in the repository.

**Naddaf, M. / Nature news feature (2025). "Is it OK for AI to write science papers? Nature survey shows researchers are split." Nature (news feature reporting a survey of ~5,000 researchers).** https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-025-01463-8 [verify exact author byline at citation time]
The best available snapshot of community norms on AI in manuscript production, reporting a Nature survey of some five thousand researchers on which uses of AI they consider acceptable and which they practise. Headline patterns per the article and secondary coverage: majorities consider AI-assisted editing of one's own text acceptable, with acceptability falling steeply for generating substantive sections, undisclosed use, and any role in peer review; adoption runs ahead of disclosure — a substantial fraction of researchers use AI assistance without declaring it — and attitudes split by career stage, region and language background, with non-native English speakers among the heaviest legitimate beneficiaries. For ch09 this evidences the normative landscape the chapter's disclosure decision flowchart must navigate: policy (theme 9's other sources) states the rules, this survey shows the community's actual, divided practice — the gap between the two is precisely why the book's provenance-record-to-disclosure-statement pipeline (ch12→ch09) is presented as the safe path through an unsettled terrain. For ch16, the career-stage and language-background splits inform the chapter's skills-and-roles discussion: agentic literacy is unevenly distributed within research groups, and adoption plans should assume contested norms rather than consensus. Caveats: journalism reporting a self-selected readership survey, not a probability sample — cite the split and its axes, not precise percentages, and date the finding (2025); attitudes in this area move quickly; and the survey concerns writing assistance broadly, not agentic workflows specifically — the book must be careful not to launder it into evidence about agents.

**National Institutes of Health (2023). "The Use of Generative Artificial Intelligence Technologies is Prohibited for the NIH Peer Review Process." NIH Guide Notice NOT-OD-23-149, 23 June 2023.** https://grants.nih.gov/grants/guide/notice-files/NOT-OD-23-149.html
The clearest example of a major funder drawing a hard line: NIH prohibits scientific peer reviewers from using LLMs or other generative AI to analyse or formulate critiques of grant applications and contract proposals, on two grounds — confidentiality (uploading application material to online AI tools is a breach of peer-review confidentiality, since the reviewer cannot control where the data goes) and the personal, non-delegable nature of the reviewer's judgement; the policy is enforced through revised reviewer security and non-disclosure agreements. For the book this notice is valuable beyond its literal scope. For ch09's reviewer-response workflows it defines a boundary condition: the manuscript's *author-side* use of agents is policy-governed but permitted with disclosure, while *evaluator-side* use may be flatly prohibited — an asymmetry the chapter should teach, since researchers occupy both roles. For ch12, the confidentiality rationale is the funder-grade articulation of the data-handling principle the chapter applies to institutional systems: material you do not control the onward use of must not enter third-party model services — exactly the data-sovereignty constraint that motivates ch14's local, open-weight tier for partners who cannot share observations. Caveats: policy class is volatile — NIH has since issued further AI-related policy (e.g. on AI-generated grant applications) [verify current state at citation time]; the prohibition reflects 2023 assumptions about consumer AI services and may evolve as secure enclaves emerge; and other funders differ (some permit disclosed AI assistance in review), so print should present the *rationale* (confidentiality plus accountability) as durable and the specific rules as repository material.

**European Commission, Directorate-General for Research and Innovation (2024; updated 8 May 2026). "Living guidelines on the responsible use of generative AI in research" (European Research Area).** Update announcement: https://research-and-innovation.ec.europa.eu/news/all-research-and-innovation-news/updated-era-living-guidelines-responsible-use-generative-ai-research-2026-05-08_en · Original guidelines PDF: https://research-and-innovation.ec.europa.eu/document/download/2b6cf7e5-36ac-41cb-aab5-0d32050143dc_en?filename=ec_rtd_ai-guidelines.pdf
The principal European funder-level guidance, addressed to researchers, research organisations and funding bodies, and explicitly maintained as a *living* document — the same versioned-update model the book itself uses, a reflexive parallel ch17 can note. The guidelines rest on four principles (reliability, honesty, respect, accountability) and operationalise them: researchers remain ultimately responsible for all scientific output and must verify AI-generated results; substantial AI use should be disclosed transparently; privacy, confidentiality and intellectual property must be protected when material is fed to AI systems; and generative AI should not be used in evaluative processes such as peer review of proposals. The May 2026 update (verified via the Commission's announcement) responds to agentic developments, adding among other things a warning about "hidden prompts" — instructions embedded in systems or content that influence model behaviour invisibly to the user — which is institutional recognition of the prompt-injection class of risk ch12 covers, bridging the security and policy themes. For ch09 and ch16 this is the European policy-class anchor complementing the US sources; its non-binding, principles-plus-updates architecture is itself the model the book recommends groups adopt internally (ch16's institutional considerations). Caveats: guidance, not law — distinct from the EU AI Act's binding requirements (not swept in depth this pass); the 2026 update's full text was verified only via the Commission's announcement page, so specific new provisions beyond those reported there should be checked against the updated PDF before quotation [verify].
