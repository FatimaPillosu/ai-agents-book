## Headline finding: the gap named in the restructure proposal has closed, and recently

The proposal recorded that no substantial peer-reviewed or preprint coverage existed for tool-surface design, context compaction policy, retry and backoff for agents, step budgeting, or observability for agent runs.
That assessment was accurate about `/research` and is no longer accurate about the literature.
Between roughly February and July 2026 the term **"harness"** itself moved from practitioner commentary into the research vocabulary: this sweep located four independent preprints and reviews that use "harness" as a technical term in their titles or abstracts (Guo et al. 2026; Zhou et al. 2026; Galster et al. 2026; Lin, M. et al. 2026), plus two more that build methods around it (Lin, J. et al. 2026; Ben Sghaier et al. 2026).
There is now a survey whose organising question is exactly the chapter's (Guo et al. 2026: "where does the bottleneck in agent performance reside, in the foundation model, in the execution harness, or in the coupling between them?").

The evidence is nonetheless **uneven across the nine proposed sections**, and its centre of gravity sits in software engineering rather than science.
Sections 4.3 (retry and error surface) and 4.5 (context and compaction) are now well evidenced with quantified studies.
Sections 4.2 (termination) and 4.4 (tool surface) are adequately evidenced.
Sections 4.6 (logging), 4.7 (human interrupts) and 4.9 (maintenance) rest on a small number of very recent preprints, several single-author.
Section 4.8 (cost and latency) has good general evidence and one directly relevant environmental-domain study.
Section 4.1 has framing sources but no case material: it needs `[AUTHOR: …]`.
The section-by-section verdict is at the end of this report and should be read before drafting.

# Theme A — The harness as an object of study (ch04 §4.1; framing for §4.9)

**Guo, J., Hao, Z., Wang, C., Fan, C., Luo, T., Li, H., Gao, Y., Mei, H., Peng, J., Xu, R., Dong, M., Wu, H., Zheng, M., Han, K., Wang, S., Xu, C. and Wang, Y. (2026). "From Question Answering to Task Completion: A Survey on Agent System and Harness Design." arXiv preprint, submitted 14 June 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.20683

This is the single most useful source located for the chapter, because its organising question is the chapter's own and it supplies a vendor-neutral decomposition the book can adopt or argue with. The authors define an LLM-based agent as "a foundation model coupled with an execution harness", then decompose that harness into **six coupled runtime responsibilities: observation, context, control, action, state and verification**. They trace four paradigms of agent engineering (prompt engineering, workflows, context engineering, harness engineering with model-harness co-evolution), analyse the limits of model-centric scaling, map task properties and domain pressures onto harness configurations, and synthesise evidence on how runtime design affects long-horizon completion, efficiency and reliability. Their closing claim is exactly the book's: agent quality "emerges from the interaction between model capability, runtime infrastructure, task structure, and evaluation design", not from the model alone. For ch04 this justifies the chapter's existence in citable form and gives §4.1 a published six-part decomposition to sit beside the book's own loop-versus-harness split. Note the mapping for ai-writer: the survey's *observation* and *action* correspond to the book's tool surface (§4.4) and error surface (§4.3), *context* to §4.5, *control* to §4.2, *state* to §4.6, and *verification* to ch11. Caveats writers must carry: it is an unrefereed survey, roughly six weeks old at the time of this sweep, from a single large industrial-academic group; surveys aggregate rather than adjudicate, so it must not be cited for any specific performance claim; and its evidence base is overwhelmingly coding and web agents. Cite it for vocabulary, for the model-versus-harness framing, and for the claim that harness design is now a recognised research object. Do not cite it for numbers.

**Zhou, C., Chai, H., Chen, W., Guo, Z., Shan, R., Song, Y., Xu, T., Yang, Y., Yu, A., Zhang, W., Zheng, C., Zhu, J., Zheng, Z., Zhang, Z., Lou, X., Zhang, C., Fu, Z., Wang, J., Liu, W., Lin, J. and Zhang, W. (2026). "Externalization in LLM Agents: A Unified Review of Memory, Skills, Protocols and Harness Engineering." arXiv preprint (technical report, 54 pages), submitted 9 April 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.08224

A 54-page review arguing that agent capability is increasingly built "less by changing model weights than by reorganizing the runtime around them". The authors trace a historical progression **from weights, to context, to harness**, and treat memory, skills and interaction protocols as three distinct but coupled forms of externalisation, with "harness engineering [as] the unification layer that coordinates them into governed execution". The word *governed* is theirs, not the book's, and the convergence is worth noting in the preface positioning argument: an independent group with no governance agenda reaches for the same term. For ch04 §4.1 this supplies the strongest available statement that the harness is where capability now comes from, and for §4.6 it supplies the useful framing that durable memory is *state externalised across time* rather than an add-on. The review also names emerging directions the book should treat carefully: self-evolving harnesses and shared agent infrastructure, both of which sit at the edge of what a research group can responsibly run. Caveats: unrefereed; a review, so no primary evidence of its own; the "cognitive artifacts" framing it borrows is a theoretical lens, not a measurement, and the book should not adopt the cognitive vocabulary given `STYLE.md`'s no-metaphor rule. The concrete content usable in print is the three-stage progression and the six-way decomposition it shares with Guo et al., both of which can be stated literally. Moderate confidence in the framing; low confidence in any specific claim about what externalisation buys, since none is measured here. For ai-writer: this and Guo et al. are the two sources available for §4.1's framing and they agree on the decomposition, so one citation carries the point and the second is redundant under the de-duplication rule.

**Galster, M., Mohsenimofidi, S., Lulla, J.L., Abubakar, M.A., Treude, C. and Baltes, S. (2026). "Harness Engineering for Agentic AI Coding Tools: An Exploratory Study." arXiv preprint, submitted 16 February 2026. Comments field states the paper is based on "Configuring Agentic AI Coding Tools: An Exploratory Study", published in the Proceedings of the 3rd ACM/IEEE International Conference on AI-powered Software (AIware 2026) [verify the proceedings version independently].** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14690

The only empirical study located of what practitioners actually put in their harnesses. The authors mined **2,853 GitHub repositories** for configuration artefacts belonging to five widely used agentic coding tools and identified **eight distinct configuration mechanisms**. Their headline findings are that context files dominate the configuration landscape, that a single filename (`AGENTS.md`) is emerging as a cross-tool interoperable standard, that few repositories adopt the more advanced mechanisms such as skills and sub-agents, and that distinct configuration cultures are forming around individual tools. For ch04 §4.1 this is the evidential answer to "what does a harness look like in the wild", and it is a useful corrective to the chapter's ambition: most real harnesses are a text file of instructions plus a permissions setting, not an engineered apparatus. That supports pitching the chapter at readers who will build something modest. Caveats, and they are substantial for this book: the study is entirely about coding tools and public GitHub repositories, so it says nothing about scientific workflows or private institutional practice; repository presence measures declared configuration, not what runs or what works; and the named tools and the `AGENTS.md` filename are products and conventions that the vendor-neutral rule keeps out of manuscript prose. The durable, citable content is the shape of the finding: configuration concentrates in a small number of mechanisms, advanced mechanisms are rare, and practice is diverging by tool. Moderate confidence, coding-domain only. For ai-writer: this is the source to reach for if §4.1 wants to say what a harness usually is rather than what it could be, and it argues for keeping the chapter's ambitions proportionate to what most readers will actually assemble, which is a text file of instructions and a permissions setting.

**Cho, Y.-e. (2026). "It's Not the Size: Harness Design Determines Operational Stability in Small Language Models." arXiv preprint, submitted 12 May 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12129

A small controlled experiment that isolates the harness variable directly, which is rare. Three harness conditions (model-only raw prompt; a minimal wrapper adding tags; and a four-stage plan-execute-verify-recover pipeline) were applied to three small language models of 2–3 billion parameters across 24 tasks, measuring task success rate and a stricter "valid" task success rate. The pipeline harness reached 0.952 task success and 1.000 valid task success on the best model over 21 of the tasks, and an ablation attributed roughly 24.7% of the total gain each to the planning stage and the recovery stage. Two secondary findings matter more to the book than the headline. First, a **non-monotonic effect**: in two of the three models the minimal wrapper scored *worse* than no harness at all, so partial scaffolding can be worse than none. Second, "scaffold collapse": one model, unsupported, abandoned its required JSON structure under complex formatting demands, producing seven format violations and a 0.429 success rate. For §4.1 and §4.9 the usable lesson is that harness benefit is not monotonic in harness effort, which is a genuinely counter-intuitive point worth making. Caveats are heavy: single author, unrefereed, 24 tasks, three small models, one benchmark family, and no confidence intervals reported in the abstract. This should be presented as a suggestive single experiment, low confidence on magnitudes, and never as evidence about the frontier models a reader would actually use. Its value is as an existence proof that harness stage design changes outcomes at fixed model. For ai-reviewer: any use of this source in the chapter should carry an explicit low-confidence flag, because a single 24-task study on three models of 2 to 3 billion parameters is weak ground for a general claim, and the book's readers will be using far larger models.

**Albayaydh, W., Zhao, R. and Flechais, I. (2026). "Beyond the Leaderboard: A Synthesis of Tool-Use, Planning, and Reasoning Failures in Large Language Model Agents." arXiv preprint (16 pages), submitted 7 July 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05775

A cross-cutting synthesis of 27 benchmark, taxonomy and audit papers published between 2023 and 2026, spanning 19 distinct benchmarks, into a single taxonomy of six failure clusters: (1) tool invocation and parameter-level errors, (2) planning and constraint-satisfaction failures, (3) long-horizon degradation from context accumulation, (4) multi-agent coordination failures, (5) safety and security failures under adversarial or underspecified conditions, and (6) measurement-validity problems. Three of its cross-literature conclusions bear directly on ch04 and should shape the chapter's tone: failures **compound non-linearly with task length**; strong performance on individual sub-tasks does **not** reliably translate into end-to-end success; and **additional scaffolding does not consistently improve reliability**. That third finding is the most important single sentence in this sweep for the chapter's honesty, because it cuts against the natural reading of a harness chapter, which is that more engineering is better. The synthesis also records real progress in single-turn tool use, short-horizon web navigation and narrowly scoped coding. For the book: cluster 1 anchors §4.4, cluster 3 anchors §4.5, cluster 6 belongs to ch11, and the non-linear compounding claim anchors §4.2's argument for step budgets. Caveats: unrefereed; a synthesis of secondary sources, so the "evidence" is other people's evidence and the taxonomy is the authors' own iterative grouping rather than a validated instrument; the 27-paper corpus is a selection whose completeness is not established; and, once again, the underlying benchmarks are software and web tasks. Cite for structure and for the three conclusions above, with a moderate-confidence flag. For ai-writer: on this evidence the formulation to avoid anywhere in ch04 is any unqualified suggestion that a better-engineered harness produces a more reliable agent.

# Theme B — Loop control: termination, budgets, drift and variance (ch04 §4.2)

**Hou, X., Wang, S., Zhao, Y. and Wang, H. (2026). "When Agents Do Not Stop: Uncovering Infinite Agentic Loops in LLM Agents." arXiv preprint, submitted 2 July 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01641

The best available evidence that missing termination conditions are a real and common defect rather than a theoretical worry, and it is unusually well suited to §4.2 because it enumerates the failure by *kind*. The authors define an Infinite Agentic Loop as a structural execution failure in which "an agent may repeatedly execute model calls, tools, workflow transitions, or agent handoffs when the feedback path is not effectively bounded", then build a static-analysis tool (IAL-Scan) that lifts heterogeneous agent codebases into a framework-agnostic representation, constructs an Agentic Loop Dependence Graph, and looks for unbounded paths to costly operations. Scanning **6,549 agent repositories** produced 74 candidate findings, of which manual review confirmed **68 genuine failures across 47 projects, a precision of 91.9%**. The breakdown is directly usable as the chapter's list of things to bound: retry feedback without bound (17 cases, 25.0%), tool-call iteration without bound (16, 23.5%), multi-agent chat without turn bound (14, 20.6%), workflow loop without effective bound (9, 13.2%), message reentry without bound (7, 10.3%), and runner, delegation or evaluator feedback (5, 7.4%). The three leading categories account for 47 of 68 failures, 69.1%. The authors' recommendations map onto the chapter's defaults almost line for line: set turn or step limits, cap retry and repair paths with timeouts, limit message-history and workflow-state size, and above all do not rely on the model eventually deciding to stop. Caveats: unrefereed; static analysis detects unbounded *paths*, not observed runaway runs, so this measures latent defect rather than realised harm; the corpus is public GitHub code of very mixed quality; and the two frameworks contributing most failures are named products that stay out of manuscript prose. High confidence in the qualitative claim, moderate in the proportions.

**Shrivastava, S. (2026). "Semantic Early-Stopping for Iterative LLM Agent Loops: A Judge-Efficient Study of When to Halt." arXiv preprint (7 pages), submitted 25 June 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.27009

This paper states the problem §4.2 exists to solve, in one sentence the chapter can paraphrase: iterative agent loops are "almost always terminated by a fixed iteration cap", which is a syntactic cut-off blind to whether the answer is still improving, so easy inputs over-spend and hard inputs get truncated. The proposed alternative halts when consecutive draft embeddings stop changing in meaning, using cosine distance with a patience window, and when measured answer quality stops improving. The contributions are a proof of deterministic termination, an evaluation method that replays stopping policies over cached model responses so different policies can be compared fairly, and an empirical result on question answering: a **38% reduction in tokens at maintained answer quality on the test set**, without needing a judge model in the loop. For ch04 §4.2 the durable content is the framing (a fixed cap is a resource guard, not a completion test, and a harness needs both) and the design idea that a no-progress condition can be measured rather than guessed. The token figure is a volatile, task-specific number and should stay out of print or carry its date. Caveats are serious: single author, unrefereed, seven pages, question-answering tasks only, and an embedding-similarity criterion that would behave differently on scientific artefacts such as code or model configurations where small textual changes carry large semantic weight. Present the *category* of stopping rule as established practice and this specific mechanism as one proposal among several, low confidence. If the manuscript wants a default, "cap plus explicit no-progress detection plus an acceptance test" is defensible from this source together with Hou et al.

**Liu, T., Wang, Z., Miao, J., Hsu, I.-H., Yan, J., Chen, J., Han, R., Xu, F., Chen, Y., Jiang, K., Daruki, S., Liang, Y., Wang, W.Y., Pfister, T. and Lee, C.-Y. (2025). "Budget-Aware Tool-Use Enables Effective Agent Scaling." arXiv preprint, submitted 21 November 2025.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.17006

The counter-intuitive result here is the one §4.2 needs when it explains why a step budget is a design parameter rather than a safety valve: **simply granting an agent a larger tool-call budget does not improve its performance**, because agents lack awareness of the budget they are spending. The authors introduce a lightweight "Budget Tracker" that gives the agent continuous visibility of remaining resources, and a scaling method (BATS) that uses that visibility to decide whether to pursue a promising line further or abandon it and try another. They also propose unified cost metrics that count both tokens and tool calls, and report improved cost-performance scaling curves. For the book, three things follow. First, budgets are part of what the agent sees, not only part of what the harness enforces, which is a genuinely useful and non-obvious design point for §4.2. Second, cost accounting that counts only tokens under-counts agent work, which reinforces ch02 §2.6's three-cost model and §4.8. Third, "give it more steps" is not a fix for a failing run, which supports the chapter's argument that budget exhaustion should be treated as a distinct termination condition with its own handler. Caveats: unrefereed; from a large industrial group with an interest in the method; evaluated on the usual tool-use benchmarks rather than scientific workflows; and the specific mechanism is more machinery than most readers of this book will build. Cite for the finding that budget-blind scaling plateaus, at moderate confidence, and keep the method itself out of print. The design consequence for §4.2 is that a budget should be visible to the run and not only enforced around it, which is a decision the reader makes and a default the chapter can state.

**Arike, R., Donoway, E., Bartsch, H. and Hobbhahn, M. (2025). "Evaluating Goal Drift in Language Model Agents." *Proceedings of the AAAI/ACM Conference on AI, Ethics, and Society (AIES)*, 8(1), 192–203.** DOI: https://doi.org/10.1609/aies.v8i1.36541 · Preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.02709

**Peer-reviewed**, and the cleanest citable evidence for the long-run failure mode the chapter must name: an agent that quietly stops pursuing the objective it was given. The design is deliberate rather than incidental. Agents are given an explicit goal in the system prompt, then exposed to environmental pressures that push a competing objective, and adherence is measured as context accumulates. The findings are that the best-performing configuration tested (a scaffolded Claude 3.5 Sonnet) maintained near-perfect goal adherence beyond 100,000 tokens in the hardest setting, that **every model evaluated showed some degree of drift**, and that drift correlates with models' increasing susceptibility to pattern-matching as context length grows. The authors also observe that drift shows up through inaction as often as through action, so an agent that simply stops doing the thing it was told to do is the characteristic form, not a dramatic deviation. For ch04 §4.2 and §4.5 this is the mechanism behind "a constraint that has fallen out of the window is a constraint the agent no longer honours" (ch02 §2.4), now with a peer-reviewed measurement behind it, and it argues for the design rule that the specification should be re-asserted periodically rather than stated once. Caveats: the setting is a simulated trading-style environment with an engineered conflict, which is not a scientific workflow; the models tested are the 2024–25 generation and absolute token thresholds will date quickly; and pattern-matching is offered as a correlate, not a demonstrated cause. Cite the structural finding, not the 100,000-token figure, which is volatile and model-specific.

**Yagubyan, A. (2026). "How Consistent Are LLM Agents? Measuring Behavioral Reproducibility in Multi-Step Tool-Calling Pipelines." arXiv preprint (16 pages), submitted April or May 2026 [verify exact date: the abstract page reports 23 April 2026, which is inconsistent with the 2605 identifier].** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.28840

Directly relevant to the book's reproducibility commitments and to why a run record (§4.6) is not optional. The study asks whether the same agent behaves the same way twice, running repeated identical invocations across six models from three providers (GPT-4.1-mini, GPT-4.1, GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet 4 and Llama 3.3 70B, with partial results for a further model) and measuring whether the same tools are selected, in the same order, with the same arguments. Agents produced **1.6 to 3.3 unique tool-call sequences per task across 10 runs**, with proprietary models clustering at 1.6–2.2 and the open-weight model at 3.3. Tool-sequence similarity averaged 0.87 (95% CI 0.84–0.90) while argument consistency averaged only 0.69 (0.64–0.74), a highly significant gap. The finding that matters most for ch04 and ch11: **structural inconsistency predicts failure** (90.2% correctness in high-similarity conditions against 61.2% in low-similarity ones), whereas argument-level variance does not (r = 0.12, not significant). So repeated-run divergence in the *shape* of a trajectory is a usable warning signal, and divergence in argument values is not. Caveats: single author, unrefereed, model line-up already ageing, and a date discrepancy that needs resolving before citation. Also note the tension with the earlier sweep's Mehta (2026) finding that silent semantic failures are *consistent* across repeated runs: agreement between runs is neither sufficient for correctness (Mehta) nor irrelevant to it (this paper). The book should present that tension rather than resolve it. For ai-writer: the practical instruction that survives every caveat here is cheap enough to recommend, which is to run the same task three times and compare the shape of the trajectories rather than only the answers. That costs model time and nothing else, and it is the kind of diagnostic a research group can adopt this week.

# Theme C — Retry, backoff and the error surface (ch04 §4.3; also ch13)

**Yang, Z. (2026). "Why Retrying Fails: Context Contamination in LLM Agent Pipelines." arXiv preprint, submitted 8 May 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08563

The best single source for the §4.3 claim that retrying an unchanged plan amplifies a wrong one, and it converts that intuition into a model with a fitted parameter. The observation is simple and, once stated, obvious: when an agent fails a multi-step task and retries, the failed attempt usually remains in its context window, so the retry runs at an elevated error rate rather than the base rate. The author formalises this as the Context-Contaminated Restart Model, a chain of T tool-call steps each failing at base rate ε₀, where any attempt following a failure operates at contaminated rate ε₁ > ε₀, and derives five results: a closed form for success within K attempts; a theorem for the extra attempts contamination costs against a clean restart; an optimal allocation of a fixed budget B = KT between pipeline depth and number of attempts; an information-theoretic lower bound; and a "clean-restart dominance" theorem quantifying the benefit of clearing context before retrying. Validation on SWE-bench Verified data is the part the book should use: the naive independence assumption **overestimates pass@3 by 17.4 percentage points (98.6% predicted against 81.2% actual)**, while the contaminated model fits with error below 0.001, implying a contamination ratio ε₁/ε₀ of 7.1. For §4.3 the design rule is explicit and durable: a retry that keeps the failed attempt in context is a different and worse operation than a retry from a clean state, so the harness must decide which it is doing. Caveats: single author, unrefereed, one validation dataset, and a two-rate model that is deliberately a simplification. The 7.1 ratio is a coding-domain fit and must not be presented as general. High confidence in the direction, low in the magnitude.

**Babu, R.S. and Agrawal, A. (2026). "Self-Healing Agentic Orchestrators for Reliable Tool-Augmented Large Language Model Systems." arXiv preprint, submitted 31 May 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.01416

A controlled fault-injection study that compares recovery strategies against each other, which is what §4.3 needs to say anything beyond "retry sometimes". The orchestrator maps observable failure signals to inferred failure classes, selects a targeted recovery action **under an explicit budget**, verifies the recovered trajectory, and records observability traces. On a 100-task fault-injection benchmark it reached 98.8% task success against 94.5% for retry-only and 93.8% for full replanning; under a matched recovery budget of a single attempt the gap widened to **94.0% against 85.3% (retry-only) and 88.2% (full replanning)**. Two findings carry into the chapter. First, *classified* recovery beats undifferentiated retry, and beats replanning-everything, which supports the §4.3 structure of asking which errors are worth retrying and what changes between attempts. Second, and more important for the book's centre of gravity, under a controlled silent-failure setting **verifier-guided self-healing reduced silent failures to 0.0% while non-verifying baselines returned wrong-but-plausible outputs more often**. That is direct support for ch02 §2.2's claim that the observe step decides everything, and it belongs in §4.3 as the argument for putting a verification step inside the recovery path rather than after it. Caveats: unrefereed, two authors, a synthetic fault-injection benchmark of 100 tasks whose realism is unestablished, and results that necessarily favour the authors' own design. The near-perfect numbers should raise the usual suspicion about benchmark construction, and the book should cite the *ordering* of the strategies rather than the absolute rates. For ai-writer: the one sentence worth carrying into §4.3 is that a recovery path without a verification step returns wrong-but-plausible output more often than one with it, which is ch11's argument arriving from the reliability direction rather than the governance direction.

**Zhu, D., Ma, X., Shen, Y., Li, X., Zhao, Y., Wang, S., Yan, L. and Yin, D. (2026). "When Tools Fail: Benchmarking Dynamic Replanning and Anomaly Recovery in LLM Agents." arXiv preprint, submitted 4 June 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05806

Existing tool-use benchmarks evaluate agents on idealised paths where tools work, and this paper builds the benchmark that does not (ToolMaze), using a two-dimensional design: graph-structured topological complexity, crossed with a 2×2 taxonomy of tool perturbations along explicit-versus-implicit and transient-versus-permanent axes. That taxonomy is itself directly usable in §4.3, because it distinguishes the four cases a retry policy must handle differently. The results are pointed. Perturbations degrade nearly every model, with **the sharpest drops under implicit semantic failures**, where the tool returns something plausible rather than an error: the perturbation recovery rate falls by around 37% in those scenarios, which the authors attribute to systemic over-trust in corrupted outputs. Complex topologies trap agents in futile trial-and-error loops, connecting this source to Hou et al. on unbounded iteration. The most consequential finding for the book's forward-looking claims is that **agentic fault tolerance improves with model scale 3.66× more slowly than basic task execution**, so recovery is a distinct bottleneck that better models are not closing at the same rate. That is a strong argument for §4.3 existing at all, and for ch17's argument about what will last. Caveats: unrefereed; a synthetic benchmark with author-designed perturbations; the 37% and 3.66× figures are benchmark-specific and should carry their measurement context if used. The durable content is the four-way perturbation taxonomy and the finding that silent, plausible tool failures are the hardest class. For ai-writer: the explicit-versus-implicit axis is the one that matters most to this book, because an explicit failure reaches the observe step and an implicit one does not. That is ch02 §2.2's central distinction restated as a benchmark design, by authors with no knowledge of the book.

**Vuddanti, S.V., Shah, A., Chittiprolu, S.K., Song, T., Dev, S., Zhu, K. and Chaudhary, M. (2025). "PALADIN: Self-Correcting Language Model Agents to Cure Tool-Failure Cases." arXiv preprint, submitted 25 September 2025. The PDF carries a header stating "Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2026"; the arXiv abstract page does not state a venue [verify].** https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25238

Included for one framing sentence and one class of evidence, not for its method. The framing: existing agent training pipelines optimise only for success trajectories, so models are never exposed during training to the tool failures that dominate real deployment, which is a clean explanation of why agents handle broken tools badly. The evidence: training on 50,000-plus recovery-annotated trajectories built by systematic failure injection, plus retrieval at inference from a bank of 55-plus failure exemplars, raised recovery rate from 32.76% to 89.68% on a ToolBench-derived evaluation, beat the strongest baseline (76.34%), and retained 95.2% of recovery performance on unseen tool APIs. For ch04 §4.3 the usable point is the **baseline**, not the improvement: an off-the-shelf agent recovering from tool failure roughly a third of the time is a number that makes the case for designing recovery deliberately. That baseline also belongs in ch13's failure gallery. Caveats, and they matter: unrefereed on arXiv with an unconfirmed venue claim; the method is fine-tuning, which is out of scope for this book's readers and must not be presented as something a research group would do; ToolBench is a synthetic API-calling corpus far from scientific tooling; and all figures are single-benchmark. Use one number at most, present it as the recovery baseline observed on a coding-adjacent benchmark, and flag the venue. For ai-reviewer: if §4.3 cites a single recovery-rate figure, this baseline is the right one, and it must appear as a description of untreated agent behaviour rather than as evidence that fine-tuning solves the problem, since fine-tuning is outside what this book asks of its readers. The venue claim should be resolved before the reference list is finalised, or the citation should read plainly as a preprint.

**Shinn, N., Cassano, F., Gopinath, A., Narasimhan, K. and Yao, S. (2023). "Reflexion: Language Agents with Verbal Reinforcement Learning." *Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 36* (NeurIPS 2023).** https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/1b44b878bb782e6954cd888628510e90-Paper-Conference.pdf · Preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366
**[verify the author list before the reference list is finalised: the NeurIPS 2023 record gives the five authors above, while the arXiv record (v4, revised 10 October 2023) gives six, adding Edward Berman between Cassano and Gopinath. Both pages were read this sweep and they disagree. Use whichever version is actually cited, and do not merge the two lists.]**

**Peer-reviewed**, foundational, and absent from `/research` until now, which is worth flagging because ch02's loop material currently rests on ReAct alone. Reflexion is the canonical statement of the pattern in which an agent, having failed, writes a verbal reflection on *why* it failed, stores that reflection in an episodic buffer, and conditions its next attempt on it, with no weight update. Its three components (an actor that acts, an evaluator that scores the outcome, and a self-reflection model that turns the score into a usable instruction) give §4.3 a citable vocabulary for "what changes between attempts", which is precisely the question the section poses. The paper reports gains across sequential decision-making, coding and language reasoning, and an ablation separating self-reflection from episodic memory alone, supporting the argument that refinement without an explicit diagnosis is weaker than refinement guided by one; a secondary summary puts that separation at roughly 8 percentage points, but this sweep verified the paper's title, authors and venue from the NeurIPS record rather than reading the ablation table, so **[verify the 8-point figure in the paper before any quantitative use]**. For the book, the durable content is architectural: an informative error surface plus a written diagnosis plus a retry is a different mechanism from a bare retry, and the difference is measurable. Caveats writers must carry: the experiments use 2023-generation models and the absolute numbers are dated; the reflection is generated by the same model that failed, which is exactly the self-grading arrangement ch11 warns against, so the book should cite Reflexion for the mechanism while noting that an external evaluator is the stronger form; and the method has a documented tendency to help most where a clear success signal exists, which is rarer in interpretive scientific work than in coding.

# Theme D — The tool surface (ch04 §4.4; also ch02 §2.3)

**Repantis, V., Gawde, A., Singh, H. and Blackwell II, J. (2026). "How Many Tools Should an LLM Agent See? A Chance-Corrected Answer." arXiv preprint (13 pages), submitted 23 May 2026, revised 7 June 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24660

The most direct evidence located for §4.4's "why fewer is usually better", and unusually careful about the measurement problem. The authors point out that showing more candidate tools mechanically raises the chance that the right tool is among them, so raw coverage rewards long lists; they therefore evaluate shortlist depth with a chance-corrected metric (Bits-over-Random) that asks whether success at a given depth beats random selection at that depth. They test across three tool-selection benchmarks and registries ranging from 20 to 3,251 tools, then reuse the metric as a reinforcement-learning reward for choosing shortlist depth per query. Two results matter. On one benchmark of 370 tools, an adaptive policy matched the coverage of showing 50 tools (90.3% against 90.8%) while showing **7 tools on average**. And in downstream validation with a frontier model, shorter adaptive lists **improved** the model's ability to pick the right tool: 93.1% against 87.1% when always shown five, widening to 76.8% against 60.9% on medium-difficulty queries where the correct tool was present but not ranked first. So the cost of a long tool list is not only tokens; it is selection accuracy. For §4.4 that supports a defensible default: expose a small, task-relevant set and retrieve the rest on demand. Caveats: unrefereed; the retrieval-shortlist framing assumes a registry large enough to need retrieval, which few scientific groups will have; the RL policy is explicitly a probe rather than a proposed system; and the named model in the downstream validation is a product and volatile. Cite the direction and the 7-versus-50 contrast, at moderate confidence, dated to May–June 2026.

**Babu, R.S. and Iyer, L.G. (2026). "ToolChoiceConfusion: Causal Minimal Tool Filtering for Reliable LLM Agents." arXiv preprint, submitted 4 June 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06284

An independent corroboration of the same finding from a different direction, which is why it is included despite overlapping with the source above. The authors argue that semantic relevance is the wrong criterion for deciding which tools to show, because a tool can be topically related to the task while being unnecessary or premature at the current step, and they propose filtering by causal sufficiency using lightweight precondition-and-effect contracts so that only the minimal next-step tool frontier is visible. The benchmark is reasonably substantial for a preprint: 102 tasks, 100 tools, four model backends and 2,448 task-method-model runs. The method matched the strongest baseline on aggregate success while **reducing visible tools from 100 to one per step and cutting token usage by about 90% against exposing all tools**. Two things transfer to §4.4 without the method. First, the failure vocabulary is precise and usable: larger tool menus increase *wrong-tool calls*, *premature actions* and token cost, and those are three distinct failures a reader can look for in their own run logs. Second, the notion that a tool's availability should depend on the state of the work, not only on its topical fit, is a durable design idea and connects to §4.7's argument that permission scope is a harness parameter. Caveats: unrefereed; two authors, one of whom also authored the self-healing orchestrator above, so these are not fully independent research programmes; the precondition contracts are hand-specified, which is real work the paper does not cost; and the 90% token figure is specific to comparing against the artificial baseline of exposing all 100 tools.

**Anthropic (2025). "Writing effective tools for AI agents — with agents." Anthropic engineering blog, 11 September 2025. Authored by Ken Aizawa with named contributors.** https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/writing-tools-for-agents

**Grey literature, vendor-authored, dated on the page.** Included because it is the most specific published guidance located on the questions §4.4 asks, and because its recommendations are testable claims rather than marketing. The substantive content: more tools do not reliably produce better outcomes, and the recommendation is a few carefully chosen tools targeting high-impact workflows; namespacing tools under common prefixes by service or by resource materially affects tool-use evaluations; descriptions and parameter names should be unambiguous, with the worked example of preferring an explicit identifier name over a bare one; **error messages returned to the agent should communicate specific, actionable improvements rather than opaque error codes or tracebacks**; tool responses should be made token-efficient through pagination, filtering, range selection and truncation with sensible defaults; and tool quality should be evaluated programmatically with simple agentic loops, tracking accuracy, runtime, tool-call counts, token consumption and error rates. The error-message recommendation is the piece §4.3 and §4.4 most need, since no peer-reviewed source located makes it as concretely. For the manuscript, the vendor-neutral rule means paraphrasing the guidance and citing it as documented industry practice, keeping the vendor's specific defaults (for example a 25,000-token response cap) out of print and in the repository if anywhere. Caveats: single-vendor, no empirical evaluation reported, experiential authority only, and its "even small refinements to tool descriptions can yield dramatic improvements" claim is unquantified and should not be repeated as stated. Mark clearly as grey literature with its September 2025 date. For ai-writer: §4.4 can carry three of its recommendations as documented industry practice, namely few tools, unambiguous names and argument schemas, and error messages written to be acted on, and should attribute them as practice rather than as evidence.

**Huang, C., Huang, X., Tran, N.P. and Milani Fard, A. (2026). "Model Context Protocol Threat Modeling and Analysis of Vulnerabilities to Prompt Injection with Tool Poisoning." *Journal of Cybersecurity and Privacy*, 6(3), article 84, published 5 May 2026.** DOI: https://doi.org/10.3390/jcp6030084

**Peer-reviewed**, and included in a harness sweep rather than left to ch12 because its finding is about the *tool declaration*, which is a §4.4 object. Applying STRIDE and DREAD threat modelling across six components of the tool-connection ecosystem, the authors enumerate 57 distinct threats and identify **tool poisoning, meaning malicious instructions embedded in tool metadata, as the highest-severity client-side vulnerability**. They then evaluate seven widely used client implementations against four tool-poisoning attack types and find significant gaps, attributed specifically to insufficient static validation of tool metadata and limited visibility of the parameters actually being passed. Their proposed defences are layered: static metadata analysis, decision-path tracking, behavioural anomaly detection and improved user transparency. For ch04 §4.4 the transferable point is that a tool's *description* is an input to the model and therefore an attack surface, which sharpens the chapter's rule that tools should be few, named clearly and reviewed, and connects it to ch12 without restating ch12's argument. For §4.7 it supports the case that parameter visibility at the approval gate is a design requirement, not a nicety. Caveats: the protocol examined is a named industry standard whose adoption and client set will change, so the manuscript must generalise to "the interface by which tools are declared to an agent"; the empirical part tests client software rather than measuring realised attacks; and severity rankings from DREAD are structured judgement, not measurement. Cite for the mechanism at moderate confidence. For ai-editor: this is a ch12 source that earns a §4.4 pointer rather than a §4.4 source in its own right. The line between the two chapters is that ch04 asks what a tool declaration says to the model and ch12 asks who is permitted to write it.

**Nguyen, D.T., Nguyen, T., Maani, F.A., Le, H.M., Sheikh, M.U., Saeed, N., Khan, M.H. and Khan, S. (2026). "TerraBench: Can Agents Reason Over Heterogeneous Earth-System Data?" arXiv preprint, submitted 11 June 2026, revised 1 July 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13148

The most valuable source in this sweep for the book's audience, because it measures tool-surface failures **in environmental science** rather than in software engineering, and because it separates process quality from answer quality. The benchmark comprises 403 agentic tasks across three tracks and eight application domains, with 24,500 verified execution steps, built on a ReAct-style executable framework that couples model planning to scientific tools for environmental retrieval, geospatial processing, simulation and artefact-backed computation. It is, the authors state, the first in this space to pair process-level tool-use metrics with tolerance-aware numeric scoring. The results are sobering and directly usable. The strongest model tested scored 59.22 on tool use but only 28.44 on numeric accuracy and 22.88 on the tolerance-aware hit rate; the strongest open-weight model reached 39.95 on tool use and 7.49 on numeric accuracy. Among failures, **numeric misses beyond tolerance ranged from 84.6% to 99.3% across models, and wrong argument values from 69.5% to 96.8%**, with wrong tool selection or ordering from 27.5% to 97.0%. The authors' conclusion is the chapter's thesis in domain form: reliable Earth-science agents must go beyond tool access to coordinate heterogeneous workflows, parameterise tools precisely and preserve artefact provenance. This is the strongest available support for ch02 §2.3's "a correct tool invoked on the wrong quantity returns an exact answer to the wrong question", and it belongs in §4.4 and ch13. Caveats: unrefereed and six weeks old; model names and scores are volatile and dated to June–July 2026; scores are benchmark-defined composites, not accuracy in any familiar sense; and the task set is the authors' construction.

# Theme E — Context assembly, retrieval and compaction (ch04 §4.5; also ch02 §2.4)

**Liu, N.F., Lin, K., Hewitt, J., Paranjape, A., Bevilacqua, M., Petroni, F. and Liang, P. (2024). "Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts." *Transactions of the Association for Computational Linguistics*, 12, 157–173.** DOI: https://doi.org/10.1162/tacl_a_00638

**Peer-reviewed**, and the citation ch02 §2.4 currently lacks. The book already asserts that material at the start and end of a long context is attended to more reliably than material in the middle, flagged as moderate confidence with the magnitude varying by model, but no source in `/research` supported it until now. This paper is that source. The authors evaluate models on multi-document question answering and on key-value retrieval, varying only the *position* of the relevant information within the input, and find that performance is often highest when the relevant material sits at the beginning or the end and degrades significantly when the model must use information in the middle, an effect present even in models explicitly built for long contexts. The paper's stated contribution is as much methodological as empirical: it supplies evaluation protocols for long-context models that vary position rather than only length. For ch04 §4.5 this converts a piece of practitioner folklore into a design rule the chapter can state plainly: where you put the specification and the acceptance criteria in the assembled context is a decision, and putting them in the middle of a long transcript is the worst available choice. Caveats: the models studied are the 2023-generation, and later architectures differ in degree though the effect has been repeatedly reproduced since; the tasks are synthetic retrieval tasks rather than agent trajectories, so the finding transfers by analogy to agent context assembly rather than directly; and the paper measures retrieval of a known fact, not the honouring of a constraint. Combine with Arike et al. (theme B) for the constraint case. High confidence in the qualitative effect, low in any specific magnitude.

**Mei, L., Yao, J., Ge, Y., Wang, Y., Bi, B., Cai, Y., Liu, J., Li, M., Li, Z.-Z., Zhang, D., Zhou, C., Mao, J., Xia, T., Guo, J. and Liu, S. (2025). "A Survey of Context Engineering for Large Language Models." arXiv preprint (166 pages, described on the page as ongoing work), submitted 17 July 2025, revised 21 July 2025.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.13334

The reference survey for §4.5's vocabulary, drawing on a stated review of more than 1,400 papers. It proposes context engineering as a discipline distinct from prompt design, "the systematic optimization of information payloads", and decomposes it into foundational components (context retrieval and generation; context processing; context management) which are then combined into system implementations (retrieval-augmented generation; memory systems and tool-integrated reasoning; multi-agent systems). Two things make it worth citing despite its bulk. First, it gives the book a published, vendor-neutral term for what §4.5 is about, so the chapter is not inventing terminology. Second, its identified research gap is a genuinely useful caution for scientific users: the survey reports an asymmetry in which models augmented by context engineering understand complex contexts well but generate correspondingly sophisticated long-form output much less well, which is a reason for ch09's insistence on human authorship of interpretation. Caveats, and they are real: this is an unrefereed and self-described ongoing work of extraordinary length, so it should be cited as a map of the field rather than as evidence for any particular technique; at 166 pages it aggregates work of very mixed quality without adjudicating; it predates the 2026 compaction literature below, so it is already partly superseded on the specific question of what to summarise away; and the generation-side asymmetry claim is the authors' synthesis rather than a single measured result. Cite for the framework and the term, at moderate confidence, and pair with the primary studies below for anything quantitative. For ai-writer: cite it once, in §4.5's opening, to establish that the term is not the book's coinage, then use the primary studies that follow for everything substantive.

**Lodha, A., Pahlavikhah Varnosfaderani, M., Chakraborty, A. and Mithal, A. (2026). "Less Context, Better Agents: Efficient Context Engineering for Long-Horizon Tool-Using LLM Agents." arXiv preprint, submitted 8 June 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.10209

The most directly usable quantitative study located for the §4.5 decision "what is resident every step, what is retrieved, what is summarised away". Four configurations were compared on a 50-task enterprise workflow benchmark, averaged over five independent runs: no user model; full conversation history; context pruned to the last five tool-call and response pairs; and pruning plus automated summarisation. The results are a clean ordering. Full-history retention reached 71.0% task completion at **1,480,996 tokens and 14.56 hours**. Pruning to the last five tool calls **improved** completion to 79.0% while cutting tokens to 535,274 and runtime to 5.39 hours. Pruning plus summarisation was best on both axes: **91.6% completion at 553,374 tokens and 5.79 hours**. The authors also report confidence intervals, effect sizes, sensitivity analysis over pruning and summary windows, a failure analysis and cross-model evidence. For the book this is the strongest single refutation of the intuition that keeping everything is safest: on this workload, keeping everything was both the most expensive and the *least* accurate configuration. That is exactly the point §4.5 needs, and it also strengthens ch02 §2.4's "treat context as a scarce, curated resource" claim, which the restructure proposal plans to compress into a pointer. Caveats: unrefereed; a single enterprise finance workflow on a named commercial platform, so the domain is remote from scientific work; the task set is 50 items; and the specific model versions date the numbers to mid-2026. Present the ordering and the mechanism, not the percentages, in print. For ai-reviewer: if §4.5 does state a number from this study, it must carry its domain, because an enterprise expense workflow is about as far from a hydrological one as this sweep could find, and a reader who transfers 91.6% to their own setting has been misled by the book rather than by the paper.

**Chen, Z., Pan, R., Dai, Y. and Netravali, R. (2026). "Slipstream: Trajectory-Grounded Compaction Validation for Long-Horizon Agents." arXiv preprint, submitted 9 May 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08580

The paper that names the risk §4.5 must not gloss: compaction is lossy in a way that cannot be checked at the moment it happens. The authors state the structural problem precisely. A compactor must condense the accumulated trajectory but is "fundamentally unaware of precisely what information the agent will need later", and because everything after compaction is conditioned on the new summary, no targeted validation criterion exists and **errors propagate silently through coherent but incorrect behaviour**. That sentence is the chapter's warning, and it converts "what that loses" from a hedge into a described mechanism. Their solution is worth one line in the book as an existence proof rather than a recommendation: run the compactor asynchronously, in parallel with continued execution on the uncompacted context, so the candidate summary and the agent's next steps are generated independently from the same state, giving a validation signal that does not depend on the summary; then have a judge check that the summary preserves both the agent's forward intent and the facts and constraints it depends on. Reported gains are up to 8.8 percentage points of task accuracy with up to 39.7% lower end-to-end latency on long-horizon coding and web-browsing workloads. Caveats: unrefereed; a systems paper whose machinery is well beyond what this book's readers will build; the judge is itself a model, so the validation inherits the reliability problem ch11 documents; and the gains are workload-specific. Cite the *problem statement* with high confidence and the mechanism at low confidence. For ai-writer: the sentence about the compactor being unaware of what the agent will need later is the §4.5 warning in its clearest published form, and it can be stated literally, without the system that follows from it.

**Zeng, W., Huang, Y. and He, J. (2026). "LOCA-bench: Benchmarking Language Agents Under Controllable and Extreme Context Growth." arXiv preprint, submitted 8 February 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07962

Evaluates the thing the chapter is about: not a model, but "a combination of models and scaffolds, including various context management strategies". The benchmark controls environment state to regulate how much context an agent accumulates, so context length can be extended in a controlled way while the underlying task semantics stay fixed, which isolates context growth from task difficulty. The two reported findings are the ones §4.5 needs: agent performance generally degrades as the environment state grows more complex, and **advanced context-management techniques can substantially improve the overall success rate**. The second half is what makes this citable for the chapter rather than only for ch11, because it is direct evidence that a harness decision, taken at fixed model, changes outcomes. The evaluation-design point is separately useful for ch11: if you want to know whether your context policy helps, hold the task fixed and vary the context, which is a study design a research group could actually run on its own workflow. Caveats: unrefereed; the abstract reports the direction of effects without magnitudes, so no number from this source should enter the manuscript; "advanced context management" is a category rather than a specified method; and the environments are simulated agent tasks rather than scientific ones. Moderate confidence in the qualitative claims, and it is best cited alongside Lodha et al. for the quantitative side. For ai-editor: this is also the sweep's best answer to a reader who asks how they would know whether their own context policy is helping. Hold the task fixed, grow the context in a controlled way, and measure success. That evaluation design transfers to a research group's own workflow even though the benchmark itself does not.

**Martin, S. and Roger, F. (2026). "Classifier Context Rot: Monitor Performance Degrades with Context Length." arXiv preprint, submitted 12 May 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12366

This is the source that ties §4.5 to the book's centre of gravity, because it shows that context degradation attacks the *checking* step, not only the working step. The authors note that monitoring an agent for dangerous behaviour means classifying transcripts that often exceed 500,000 tokens, while prior monitoring benchmarks rarely contain transcripts longer than 100,000. Testing three frontier models as classifiers on a dataset requiring them to spot a coding agent taking a subtly dangerous action, they find the models **miss those actions 2× to 30× more often when the action occurs after 800,000 tokens of benign activity than when it occurs alone**. Prompting mitigations, notably periodic reminders inserted through the transcript, partially recover performance. Their conclusion is directly transferable: monitor evaluations that ignore long-context degradation overestimate monitor performance. For the book, this belongs in §4.5 as the reason context policy is a verification concern and not only a cost concern, in §4.6 as an argument for structuring the run record so a reviewer does not have to read a 500,000-token transcript, and in ch11 as a measured false-negative mechanism for automated review gates, which the first sweep recorded as a genuine gap. Caveats: unrefereed; the models named are current products and the 2×–30× range is dated to May 2026 and will move; the task is adversarial-action detection in coding transcripts, so the transfer to scientific review is structural rather than numerical; and the periodic-reminder mitigation is reported as partial, not solved. For ai-editor: this source bears on two chapters, and the de-duplication rule argues that ch11 owns the false-negative argument about review gates while §4.5 keeps only the claim that context growth degrades checking as well as doing.

**Xia, S., Wang, Y., Huang, Z. and Liu, P. (2026). "Diagnosing and Mitigating Context Rot in Long-horizon Search." arXiv preprint, submitted 29 June 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29718

Adds a failure mode to the context-degradation picture that the book has not yet named, and it is a behavioural one rather than an accuracy one. Evaluating four flagship open-weight models across three benchmarks in deep-search settings, the authors report a "prevalent but unnoticed" pattern: as context accumulates, models **give up outright or prematurely commit to an uncertain answer**, and the tendency worsens as context grows. Pruning experiments link the behaviour to accumulated context rather than to task difficulty. The paper then does the systematic comparison §4.5 needs, evaluating seven context-management methods in three categories on performance, cost and effect on context rot, and reporting explicit guidance for choosing between them; it separately develops a rot-aware rejection-sampling filter and shows the two approaches compose. For the book, the durable content is that context exhaustion does not only degrade quality, it changes the agent's disposition towards finishing, which is a distinct thing for a reader to watch for and a distinct reason to bound run length. It also complicates any naive reading of a stop condition: an agent that stops may have stopped because it gave up. Caveats: unrefereed and four weeks old at the sweep date; open-weight models only, so behaviour on closed frontier models is unmeasured here; deep-search tasks only; and the seven-method comparison is summarised in the abstract without figures, so the specific guidance would need reading the full paper before any claim is made about which method to prefer. For ai-writer: the giving-up behaviour deserves a distinct entry in ch13's failure gallery, because a run that abandons the task looks from the outside like a run that finished, and no completion signal distinguishes them.

**Anthropic (2025). "Effective context engineering for AI agents." Anthropic engineering blog, 29 September 2025. Authored by the Applied AI team: Prithvi Rajasekaran, Ethan Dixon, Carly Ryan and Jeremy Hadfield, with named contributors.** https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents

**Grey literature, vendor-authored, dated on the page.** The clearest single articulation of the three strategies §4.5 needs to name, and the one place they are set out together. Compaction: summarise the conversation as it approaches the context limit and restart from the compressed summary, preserving decisions, unresolved problems and implementation detail while discarding redundant tool output, and tune for recall first and precision second. Structured note-taking: have the agent write notes to persistent storage outside the context window and read them back when needed, giving durable memory at low overhead. Sub-agents: delegate exploration to agents with their own clean context which return only a distilled summary, with the guide reporting that a sub-agent may consume tens of thousands of tokens and return 1,000 to 2,000. That last point is exactly ch02 §2.5's delegation-as-context-decision argument, independently stated, and §4.5 can point to it rather than re-deriving. The guide also gives the tool-curation rule §4.4 uses: if a human engineer cannot say definitively which tool applies in a given situation, an agent cannot be expected to do better. Caveats: single-vendor, experiential, no evaluation reported, and both its illustrative numbers are product-specific and volatile. Under the vendor-neutral rule, paraphrase the three strategies as documented industry practice and keep the figures in the repository. Its main value is that it names the strategies crisply enough for a chapter to structure around, with the primary studies above supplying the evidence. For ai-editor: since a different Anthropic engineering post is already cited in ch02 §2.5, adding two more to ch04 would give the reference list a visible vendor pattern that sits awkwardly with the vendor-neutral rule, and paraphrase plus a single citation is the safer route.

# Theme F — State, memory, logging and the run record (ch04 §4.6; also ch12)

**Dong, L., Lu, Q. and Zhu, L. (2024). "AgentOps: Enabling Observability of LLM Agents." arXiv preprint (12 pages), submitted 8 November 2024, revised 30 November 2024.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.05285

The earliest systematic attempt located to answer §4.6's question "what should a run write down", and still the most reference-like. Working from a DevOps framing, the authors argue that observability is a precondition for safety, because it is what lets stakeholders understand an agent's inner workings, detect anomalies and pre-empt failures rather than diagnose them afterwards. They then derive, from a systematic mapping study of existing observability tools, **a taxonomy of the artefacts and associated data that should be traced across the whole agent lifecycle**, offered explicitly as a reference template for designing logging and analytics infrastructure. For ch04 §4.6 this is the citable backing for the claim that there is a definable answer to what to record, and that it is not simply "everything". It is also the cleanest source for the chapter's stated distinction between the diagnostic record (§4.6) and the accountability record (ch12), since AgentOps is explicitly about the former: the framing is detect, diagnose, prevent, not attest. Caveats: unrefereed; from late 2024, so it predates the agent architectures and the telemetry standardisation effort of 2026 and its tool survey is stale; taxonomies of this kind are descriptive and unvalidated, with no evidence offered that conforming systems are safer or easier to debug; and the DevOps framing assumes an engineering organisation rather than a research group, so the book must translate. Cite for the existence and shape of a recording taxonomy, at moderate confidence, and pair with Wang et al. below for the 2026 state of the art.

**Wang, Y., Zhang, J., Cai, T., Liu, Z., Sun, Q., Sun, Z., Wu, Z., Dong, M., Zheng, M., Yin, X. and Zhu, Y. (2026). "From Agent Traces to Trust: A Survey of Evidence Tracing and Execution Provenance in LLM Agents." arXiv preprint, submitted 3 June 2026, latest revision 28 June 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.04990

The most complete current treatment of the run record, and the one that connects §4.6 to ch12 in the book's own terms. The survey's opening argument is the book's argument: "Final-answer accuracy alone cannot explain how an output was produced, which evidence supported each claim, whether tool calls were justified, how memory influenced later decisions, or where failures originated." It defines execution provenance as the typed graph of an agent execution and evidence tracing as its projection onto evidence-support relations, then builds a taxonomy over trace sources, evidence and execution units, provenance relations, tracing granularity and timing, representation forms, and trust functions. It reviews methodological directions including provenance representation, evidence attribution, tool-use provenance, runtime guardrails, provenance-bearing memory, observability and failure diagnosis, and closes on benchmarks, metrics and open challenges for auditable and recoverable agents. For the book this is the strongest single citation for ch12's audit-trail argument and for §4.6's claim that a run record has a structure. The granularity axis is directly useful: run-level tracing supports coarse audit and reproducibility but is insufficient for claim-level verification or failure localisation, which is precisely the distinction the chapter draws between its own record and ch12's. Caveats: unrefereed; a survey, so no primary evidence; four revisions in under a month indicates active churn; and it is a security-venue survey, so its trust framing is adversarial where the book's is mostly about honest error. For ai-writer: the granularity distinction is the part §4.6 needs most, because it separates the record that lets someone re-run a workflow from the record that lets someone find the step where it went wrong. The chapter's whole argument is that these are different artefacts with different retention needs, and this survey states the distinction in citable form.

**Ding, T., Nannapaneni, A., Liu, B. and Zhang, L. (2026). "Always-On Agents: A Survey of Persistent Memory, State, and Governance in LLM Agents." arXiv preprint, submitted 29 June 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30306

The best source for the §4.6 material on state that persists between runs, and it contains a finding the book should quote in substance because it validates the whole governance-first stance. Treating always-on agents as persistent-state systems, the authors include in "state" not only retrievable memories but task ledgers, permissions, credentials, commitments, provenance and audit records, shared state, trigger conditions and externally committed effects, and they read the literature through six diagnostic axes per state item: authority, scope, mutability, provenance, recoverability and actionability. The lifecycle they propose (state is written, validated, organised, retrieved, acted upon, updated, forgotten, audited and sometimes rolled back) is a usable checklist for a reader designing what their harness keeps. Across a coded corpus of **435 works**, they report that the literature concentrates far more heavily on **accumulating and retrieving state than on governing, recovering or relinquishing it**, and they respond with a pilot evaluation protocol that scores state mutation and recovery obligations rather than answer quality. For the book, that imbalance is a citable, quantified statement that the field under-studies exactly what this book foregrounds, which strengthens the positioning claim in the preface. Caveats: unrefereed and under a month old; the corpus is explicitly described by the authors as a scoped map rather than an exhaustive census, so the imbalance is indicative rather than measured; the evaluation protocol is a v0 pilot with no reported use; and the framing assumes long-lived deployed agents, which is not most scientific practice. For ai-writer: the six diagnostic axes make a usable checklist for what a harness should keep between runs, and the nine-stage lifecycle supplies §4.6 with a structure that is not the book's invention.

**Souza, R., Gueroudji, A., DeWitt, S., Rosendo, D., Ghosal, T., Ross, R., Balaprakash, P. and Ferreira da Silva, R. (2025). "PROV-AGENT: Unified Provenance for Tracking AI Agent Interactions in Agentic Workflows." *Proceedings of the 2025 IEEE 21st International Conference on e-Science*, Chicago, IL, USA.** Preprint and authors' own citation string: https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.02866 · **[verify: the IEEE DOI could not be confirmed this sweep; IEEE Xplore was not reachable.]**

**Peer-reviewed** (conference), and the closest thing located to a run record designed for science rather than for software operations, which makes it the right citation for the §4.6 pointer to ch12. The authors extend the W3C PROV standard with a provenance model tailored to agentic workflows, implement a near-real-time open-source capture system, and evaluate it across edge, cloud and high-performance-computing facilities, demonstrating support for provenance queries and agent-reliability analysis. Two features matter to this book specifically. First, the choice to extend an existing, widely adopted provenance standard rather than invent a schema is exactly the advice ch12 gives research groups, and it means an agent run can be recorded in the same vocabulary as the rest of a computational workflow. Second, the authors' stated motivation is hallucination risk propagating through multi-step workflows, so the record is designed to let a later reader trace a wrong output back to the step that produced it, which is §4.6's diagnostic purpose. The cross-facility evaluation, including HPC, is unusually relevant to environmental modelling groups. Caveats: the evaluation demonstrates that queries are supported, not that scientists find the records usable, and no user study is reported; the system is one implementation among several; the venue is a specialist e-Science conference rather than a mainstream ML venue; and the DOI needs confirming before the reference list is finalised. For ai-writer: this is the source that lets §4.6 point to ch12 without restating it, because it is the only located work that treats an agent run record as a scientific-provenance artefact rather than as operational telemetry. For ai-editor: it is also the strongest candidate for `FURTHER-READING.md` under a harness and loop section, since a research group wanting to build a run record could start from it and from an existing provenance standard rather than from nothing.

**Hajimiri, S., Aminbeidokhti, M., Dolz, J., Ben Ayed, I., Laradji, I.H., Gella, S. and Gontier, N. (2026). "Are Online Skill and Memory Modules Always Worth Their Tokens? A Budget-Constrained Study of Web Agents." arXiv preprint, submitted 12 June 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.15017

A discipline-imposing result that the chapter should carry into both §4.6 and §4.8, because it is the strongest available caution against adding harness machinery on faith. Memory, workflow and skill modules are usually evaluated against a baseline that does not spend the tokens those modules consume. The authors instead compare three published augmentation methods against **a token-matched vanilla baseline that spends the same budget on additional actor steps**, across WebArena domains, three language models, and a second benchmark. The baseline "frequently matches or surpasses all three augmentation methods in aggregate success rate", and the authors conclude that skill and workflow memory have domain-specific utility whose apparent gains "often vanish against a budget-matched actor". They further argue that run-to-run variance should be a core evaluation metric, which converges with Yagubyan (theme B) from an unrelated direction. For the book, the transferable rule is that any harness component must be evaluated against spending the same resources on simply letting the agent work longer, and that is a fair, cheap comparison a research group can actually run. It is also a useful corrective to the enthusiasm of the memory literature. Caveats: unrefereed; web-navigation benchmarks only; three specific augmentation methods rather than the category as a whole; and "frequently matches or surpasses" is a summary of mixed results, so the honest statement is that the gains are not robust, not that memory modules do not work. For ai-reviewer: this source should be invoked wherever ch04 recommends adding a harness component, as the standing test of whether that component earns the tokens it costs. It is the cheapest piece of methodological advice in this sweep and the easiest for a research group to apply.

# Theme G — Human interrupts, approval gates, permissions and sandboxing (ch04 §4.7; also ch12 §12.8)

**Turan, E. (2026). "Oversight Has a Capacity: Calibrating Agent Guards to a Subjective, Fatiguing Human." arXiv preprint (12 pages), submitted 8 June 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08919

The most useful source located for §4.7 because it attacks the assumption the section is built on. The standard pattern is an approval gate that pauses risky actions for a person, and the author's argument is that "the gate is the easy part; the hard part is the judgment", which the field evaluates against two false assumptions: that "risky" has a ground truth, and that the reviewer is a perfect, always-available oracle. On a hand-labelled set of 125 adversarially weighted agent actions, three results follow. Reviewers agree only moderately on what counts as risky (**Fleiss' κ = 0.52**), so there is no single correct label to calibrate against. Framing the guard as selective classification under asymmetric cost makes its operating limits measurable, and on hard inputs the guard cannot safely decide alone. And when the reviewer is modelled as fatiguing under load, realised safety becomes an **inverted U in the escalation rate**, so more human oversight can make a system less safe, and the safety-optimal policy escalates below full escalation. A load-aware policy also resists a flooding attack that slips a malicious action past a tired reviewer. For the book this is the citable basis for §4.7's rule that pause points are chosen by consequence rather than by capability, and for the batching-approvals guidance. Caveats, stated candidly by the author: the inverted-U and the flooding attack are **modelling results motivating a human study, not measured human behaviour**; the mechanisms are acknowledged prior art from the learning-to-defer literature; single author, unrefereed, 125 items. The κ figure is a real measurement; the fatigue curve is not. The book must keep that distinction.

**Wang, P., Li, Y. and Tian, Y. (2026). "Reframing LLM Agent Security as an Agent-Human Interaction Problem." arXiv preprint, submitted 23 May 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24309

Supplies the empirical picture of what deployed systems actually do at the human boundary, which §4.7 needs so it can describe practice rather than prescribe in a vacuum. Surveying **59 academic papers, 21 production agent systems and 26 security plugins as of April 2026**, the authors find that three human-centric mechanisms dominate industry practice, each adopted by at least 14 of the 21 production systems: **policy specification (14), runtime approval (15) and scope configuration (16)**. The mechanisms most studied in academia, intent anchoring and trust labelling, have **zero production deployment**. They also name the trade-off §4.7 must handle honestly: current human-participation mechanisms leave users caught between approval fatigue and uncontrolled autonomy, with no good operating point. For ch04 the usable content is the three-mechanism vocabulary, which maps cleanly onto the chapter's structure (what you write down in advance, what you approve at run time, what the run may reach at all) and lets §4.7 point at ch12 §12.8 for the third without restating it. The industry-academia mismatch is separately a good line for ch17. Caveats: unrefereed; explicitly a position paper, so the framing is argumentative; "production systems" means commercial coding and computer-use agents, a set whose composition changes monthly and whose names cannot appear in print; and counts of adoption are the authors' classification of documented behaviour, not audits. Dated April 2026; treat the counts as a snapshot. For ai-writer: the three mechanisms give §4.7 a structure that needs no product name, because policy specification is what ch03 already calls the specification, runtime approval is the pause point the section is about, and scope configuration is the permission boundary ch12 §12.8 argues for. The section can therefore point in both directions without restating either.

**Stanley, R., Verma, A., Tsai, L., Kallas, K. and Kumar, S. (2026). "An AI Agent Execution Environment to Safeguard User Data." arXiv preprint, submitted 21 April 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19657

Included for one structural idea that §4.7 can use without adopting the system: the execution environment can enforce a permission specification **deterministically, without trusting the model at all**. The authors present GAAP, an environment that collects permission specifications from the user through directed prompts describing how private data may be shared, then enforces that the agent's disclosures comply, including disclosures to the model and its provider. Enforcement uses information-flow control augmented with persistent stores and annotations that track private data both within a single task and across tasks separated in time. The stated guarantee is that it holds without requiring the model or the prompt to be free of attacks, and the evaluation reports that all tested data-disclosure attacks were blocked, including attacks that made comparable systems leak, without a significant loss of agent utility. For the book, the transferable point is a clean design distinction the chapter can make in one sentence: a permission enforced by the harness is categorically different from a permission the model has been asked to respect, and only the first survives an adversary or a confused model. That is the general form of ch12 §12.8's least-privilege argument, which §4.7 should point to rather than repeat. Caveats: unrefereed; the threat model is personal-data confidentiality for consumer assistants, not scientific data governance; the system requires the user to author a permission specification, which is unglamorous work the paper does not cost; "without significant impact on utility" is measured on the authors' own workloads; and the machinery is far beyond what a research group would deploy. Cite the principle, not the system.

# Theme H — Cost and latency control (ch04 §4.8; also ch02 §2.6, ch16)

**Bouchard, D. (2026). "Is Escalation Worth It? A Decision-Theoretic Characterization of LLM Cascades." arXiv preprint, submitted 7 May 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.06350

The most rigorous source located on §4.8's "cheaper models for cheap steps", and its conclusion is more sceptical than the practitioner consensus, which is exactly why it is worth citing. Cascades, in which a cheap model defers to an expensive one on low-confidence queries, are usually tuned by picking a deferral threshold empirically. The author develops a decision-theoretic framework, establishes the shape of the cost-quality frontier for a two-model cascade, characterises the frontier achievable from a pool of k models as the envelope over pairwise cascades, and derives first-order conditions in which a single shadow price equalises marginal quality per unit cost across stage boundaries. Validation spans five benchmarks and eight models from five providers. Two empirical findings matter to the book. Full fixed chains **underperform** the simple pairwise envelope, and optimised longer cascades do not deliver meaningful held-out gains over it, so more stages is not better. And a lightweight **pre-generation router beat the best cascade policy on four of five datasets**, mainly because a cascade must pay the cheap model's generation cost before it can decide to escalate, so cascade performance is limited by structural cost rather than by routing signal quality. For §4.8 the usable rule is that deciding *before* generating is cheaper than deciding after, and that two tiers are usually enough. Caveats: single author, unrefereed; benchmarks are question-answering, mathematics and coding, not agent trajectories, so the transfer to multi-step agent runs is by analogy; and model line-ups date fast. Cite the structural conclusions, not the model comparisons.

**Bao, A., He, J., Hsu, A., Manya, D. and Zhang, J. (2026). "The Energy Footprint of LLM-Based Environmental Analysis: LLMs and Domain Products." arXiv preprint, submitted 31 March 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00053

The only source located that measures agentic cost in the currency this book's readers care about most, and it is set in the environmental domain. The authors assess inference-time energy consumption of two climate-analysis chatbots against a generic small model, decomposing each workflow into retrieval, generation and hallucination-checking components, estimating energy from actual user queries, and testing across times of day and access locations. The finding §4.8 needs: energy use depends strongly on workflow design, and **more agentic pipelines substantially increase inference-time energy, particularly where the extra steps are accuracy or verification checks, without proportional gains in output quality**. Reported secondary detail indicates the verification-heavy system consumed roughly 45 times the energy of the simpler cosine-similarity approach, though that figure comes from search-result summary rather than from the abstract and should be **read from the paper before use [verify]**. For the book this is important and awkward in equal measure, because it puts a real environmental cost against the verification-first stance ch02 §2.6 and ch11 argue for. The honest reading, and the one the chapter should take, is that verification is not free and its cost is not only financial, so gates should be placed where they change decisions rather than everywhere. That is a stronger argument than pretending the trade-off does not exist. Caveats: unrefereed; two specific chatbots; energy is estimated from tokens and runtime rather than measured at the device; the authors themselves call the findings initial and call for more robust testing. For ai-editor: this is the one genuinely uncomfortable finding in the sweep for the book's own thesis, and ch04 is stronger for naming it than for leaving it out, particularly given the readership.

# Theme I — Maintaining a harness across model and framework change (ch04 §4.9)

**Ben Sghaier, O., Li, H., Adams, B. and Hassan, A.E. (2026). "Don't Blame the Large Language Model: How Agent Harness Evolution Shapes Coding Agent Quality." arXiv preprint, submitted 4 July 2026, revised 20 July 2026. [The title changed between versions; search indexes still show the v1 title "How Scaffolding Evolution Shapes Coding Agent Quality". Cite the v2 title.]** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.03691

The single best source for §4.9, because it does the controlled experiment the section's argument needs: hold the model constant and vary only the harness, over time. The authors evaluated **35 sequential releases** of one open-source agent command-line tool against 50 tasks with the language model fixed. Resolve rate showed **no statistically significant improvement across releases** (Spearman ρ = 0.208, p = 0.231), fluctuating around a mean of 30.5% between roughly 23% and 39%, with early versions sometimes beating later ones. Token consumption meanwhile rose strongly and monotonically (ρ = 0.743, p < 0.0001), from about 391,000 to about 668,000 tokens, a 70% increase with no matching effectiveness gain; failed tasks consumed 2.7 times the tokens of successful ones. Release velocity across five systems ranged from 1.5 to 18.0 releases per week, 13 to 28 times more release-intensive than mainstream developer tools. Mapping commits to a ten-component reference architecture, the authors found regression risk concentrated in the model-provider layer and in **context management**, while extensibility and security components were comparatively safe to change. For the book: this is quantified support for scheduled reappraisal, for treating harness upgrades as changes requiring re-evaluation, and for the specific warning that context-management changes are the risky ones. Caveats: unrefereed; one tool for the longitudinal part; 50 coding tasks; named products throughout, so print must generalise; the figures are dated July 2026. For ai-writer: the finding that context-management changes carry the highest regression risk is the actionable content, because it tells a reader which part of their own harness to re-test first after any upgrade. That is a better §4.9 default than any calendar interval, and it is the only such default this sweep can support from evidence.

**Lin, M., Wu, J., Wang, Z., Shi, Z., Sang, Y., He, B., Liu, Z., Wei, T., Wu, Z., Zhang, Z., Wang, D., Zhang, X., Dumoulin, B., Xie, C., Zhou, Y., Wang, S. and Lu, H. (2026). "Harness Updating Is Not Harness Benefit: Disentangling Evolution Capabilities in Self-Evolving LLM Agents." arXiv preprint (24 pages), submitted 28 May 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30621

Directly addresses the §4.9 scenario of a harness built around a weaker model's limitations, and produces two findings that are counter-intuitive enough to be worth the chapter's space. The authors separate two capabilities: **harness-updating**, producing useful persistent updates to prompts, skills, memories and tools from execution evidence, and **harness-benefit**, actually performing better when given an updated harness. First finding: harness-updating is **flat in base capability**, with models from very different capability tiers producing updates that yield surprisingly similar gains, including a small open-weight model matching a frontier model. Second finding: harness-benefit is **non-monotonic**, with weak models gaining little, mid-tier models gaining most, and strong models gaining less than mid-tier. Two failure modes explain the weak tier: the model either fails to activate the relevant harness artefact at all, or activates it and then fails to follow it. Their practical recommendation is to spend capability budget on the agent doing the task rather than on the agent maintaining the harness. For the book this supports the §4.9 argument that harness effort and model capability interact rather than add, and it converges independently with Cho (theme A) on non-monotonicity. Caveats: unrefereed; a large industrial author list with an associated code release; results are benchmark-derived and the tier boundaries are the authors' own; "self-evolving harness" is a research setting, not something a research group should run, and the book should extract the finding without endorsing the practice. For ai-writer: read alongside Cho, these two sources support one durable claim for §4.9, which is that harness effort and model capability interact non-monotonically, so a harness tuned around one model's weaknesses is not automatically better when a stronger model replaces it.

**Lin, J., Liu, S., Pan, C., Lin, L., Dou, S., Xi, Z., Huang, X., Yan, H., Han, Z., Gui, T. and Jiang, Y.-G. (2026). "Agentic Harness Engineering: Observability-Driven Automatic Evolution of Coding-Agent Harnesses." arXiv preprint, submitted 28 April 2026, latest revision 18 May 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25850

Included for one methodological idea and one ablation, both of which serve §4.9 and §4.6 without the book needing to endorse automated harness evolution. The methodological idea is that **every harness edit is paired with a self-declared prediction of its effect, which is then verified against the next round's outcomes**, turning each change into a falsifiable claim rather than an act of faith. That is a governance mechanism a research group could adopt manually, and it is the clearest expression found anywhere in this sweep of how to maintain a harness responsibly: write down what you expect the change to do, then check. The authors also give every editable harness component a file-level representation so changes are explicit and revertible, which is version control applied to the harness. Reported results: ten iterations raised pass@1 on one benchmark from 69.7% to 77.0%, exceeding a human-designed harness at 71.9%; the frozen result transferred to a second benchmark with 12% fewer tokens, and gave cross-family gains of 5.1 to 10.1 percentage points on three other model families. The ablation is the part to cite: gains localised to **tools, middleware and long-term memory rather than the system prompt**, suggesting that structural harness content transfers between models while prose-level strategy does not. Caveats: unrefereed; coding benchmarks; an automated-evolution system well outside the book's scope; and results from the authors' own pipeline. Cite the prediction-and-verification discipline and the ablation, not the numbers. For ai-editor: the discipline of pairing every harness edit with a written prediction and then checking it is close enough to the book's own reflexive-production stance to be worth a sentence in ch17 as well as in §4.9.

# Theme J — Domain anchors and evidence that loop design changes outcomes (ch04 worked example; ch06, ch08, ch11, ch14)

**Kapoor, S., Stroebl, B., Kirgis, P., Nadgir, N., Siegel, Z.S., Wei, B., Xue, T., Chen, Z., Chen, F., Utpala, S., Ndzomga, F., Oruganty, D., Luskin, S., Liu, K., Yu, B., Arora, A., Hahm, D., Trivedi, H., Sun, H., Lee, J., Jin, T., Mai, Y., Zhou, Y., Zhu, Y., Bommasani, R., Kang, D., Song, D., Henderson, P., Su, Y., Liang, P. and Narayanan, A. (2025). "Holistic Agent Leaderboard: The Missing Infrastructure for AI Agent Evaluation." arXiv preprint, submitted 13 October 2025.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.11977

This is the follow-on infrastructure from the same group as *AI Agents That Matter* (already in the first sweep), and it is the strongest evidence located that the harness is a first-class experimental variable rather than an implementation detail. The stated contributions are standardised evaluation infrastructure that shortens an evaluation from weeks to hours, **three-dimensional analysis across models, scaffolds and benchmarks**, and model-assisted inspection of run logs. Scale: 21,730 agent rollouts across 9 models and 9 benchmarks, roughly US$40,000 of compute, with 2.5 billion tokens of model calls released publicly. The log inspection surfaced behaviours no leaderboard would show, including agents searching for the benchmark itself online instead of solving the task, and misusing payment details in booking tasks. For ch04 the citable claim is that scaffold is a dimension of variation on a par with model choice, which is the chapter's premise. For ch11 the log-inspection finding is a strong argument that aggregate scores hide the behaviours a scientist would most want to know about, and it reinforces §4.6's case for keeping inspectable records. Caveats: unrefereed as verified here; the released token volume and rollout counts are impressive but the benchmarks remain coding, web and tool-use tasks; the very large author list spans institutions with differing interests; and the log-inspection findings are illustrative anecdotes drawn from automated review, not a measured failure rate. For ai-editor: this is the sweep's cleanest justification for ch04 existing as a chapter rather than as a section, because it treats the scaffold as a dimension of experimental variation on a par with the model, and it does so at a scale no individual group could reach. For ai-writer: cite it for that framing, and leave the leaderboard results themselves to ch11, which already owns the benchmark-validity argument.

**Nguyen et al. (2026), TerraBench — see theme D above.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13148
Cross-referenced here because it is the domain anchor for the chapter's worked example as well as the tool-surface evidence. Its three-track structure (fundamentals, simulator-grounded, document-grounded verification) and its pairing of process-level tool-use metrics with tolerance-aware numeric scoring together give ch04 and ch11 a concrete template for what evaluating an environmental-science agent looks like when you care about how it got there and not only what it answered. The failure profile it reports, dominated by numeric misses and wrong argument values rather than by wrong tool choice at the frontier, is the single most chapter-relevant domain finding in this sweep: for Earth-system work, the tool surface fails mostly at parameterisation, which is precisely what §4.4 should tell readers to constrain and what §4.6 should tell them to log.

**Feng, P., Lv, Z., Ye, J., Wang, X., Huo, X., Yu, J., Xu, W., Zhang, W., Bai, L., He, C. and Li, W. (2026). "Earth-Agent: Unlocking the Full Landscape of Earth Observation with Agents." Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2026. Preprint submitted 27 September 2025.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.23141

**Peer-reviewed** (ICLR 2026), and the domain instance of the §4.4 problem at scale. Earth-Agent is a ReAct-style agentic framework unifying RGB and spectral Earth-observation data within a tool ecosystem, built to support tasks such as geophysical parameter retrieval and quantitative spatiotemporal analysis by dynamically invoking expert tools and models across modalities. The accompanying benchmark, Earth-Bench, comprises 248 expert-curated tasks over 13,729 images spanning spectral, product and RGB modalities, with a **dual-level evaluation protocol that assesses both the reasoning trajectory and the final outcome**. Experiments vary the model backbone and compare against general-purpose agent frameworks and against multimodal models on remote-sensing benchmarks. For ch04 this matters in three ways. It is a peer-reviewed demonstration that a large, curated, domain-specific tool surface is the thing that makes an Earth-observation agent possible, which grounds §4.4 in the reader's own field. Its dual-level protocol is a second independent instance of the process-plus-outcome evaluation design that TerraBench also adopts, which strengthens ch11's argument that trajectory evaluation is now standard practice in this domain. And the authors' framing of "expert tools" as the unit of capability is exactly ch02 §2.3's delegated-weakness argument in Earth-observation terms. Caveats: the tool count and ecosystem are product-linked and volatile; the tasks are remote-sensing analysis rather than modelling or forecasting, so the transfer to hydrology and meteorology is partial; and, as with all such frameworks, the authors evaluate their own system. For ai-writer: this is the peer-reviewed domain citation §4.4 should prefer if it wants only one, since TerraBench is a preprint and this is not, and the two agree independently on evaluating the trajectory as well as the outcome.

**Yan, S., Li, Z., Zhu, S., Wen, Y., Zhang, M., Chen, M., Cao, J. and Hong, Y. (2025). "AQUAH: Automatic Quantification and Unified Agent in Hydrology." Preprint, ICCV 2025 SEA workshop paper (8 pages), submitted 4 August 2025.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.02936

Included because it is the closest published analogue to the operational hydrology work the book draws on, and because its honesty about limits is useful. AQUAH takes a natural-language prompt, retrieves terrain, forcing and gauge data, configures a hydrologic model, runs the simulation and generates a self-contained report, with vision-enabled models interpreting maps and rasters and steering outlet selection, parameter initialisation and uncertainty commentary. Initial experiments across a range of US basins showed cold-start simulations completed and analyst-ready documentation produced without manual intervention, and hydrologists judged the results clear, transparent and physically plausible. The authors state plainly that further calibration and validation are needed before operational deployment. For ch04 the value is as a domain reference point for §4.1's concrete case and for the chapter's tool-surface discussion: the tools here are data retrieval, model configuration, simulation execution and figure generation, which is a recognisable list for the book's readers. It also supports ch08's framing. Caveats, and they should be stated wherever it is cited: a workshop paper rather than a full peer-reviewed article; "judged by hydrologists as plausible" is expert impression, not validation against observations; no failure analysis and no verification gates are reported, so it illustrates what an agent can be made to do rather than what can be trusted; and the specific model (CREST) and basins are the authors' choices. For ai-editor: AQUAH is the closest published work to the book's own subject matter located in three sweeps, and the absence of any verification gate, permission boundary or run record from its description is itself an argument for ch04 existing. Cite it as a capability demonstration and say plainly what it does not report, which is the more useful half.

**Wang, J., Lv, W., Fu, B., Fu, J., Song, J., Zhang, L., Xue, L., Chen, L., Xin, Z., Li, K. and Cao, X. (2026). "Act As a Real Researcher: A Suite of Benchmarks Evaluating Frontier LLMs and Agentic Harnesses in Research Lifecycle." arXiv preprint, submitted 5 June 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.07462

The most useful dissenting source in the sweep, and the one the chapter should use to close. Where every other harness source argues that runtime design determines outcomes, these authors evaluate frontier models and agentic harnesses on research-intern-level tasks that demand professionalism, thoroughness and nuanced judgement rather than macro-level execution, and report that the **best configuration reached only 68.3% success**, frequently overlooking subtle but critical details that are obvious to a human researcher. Their conclusion is explicit and cuts against the chapter's own enthusiasm: developing researcher-like AI "requires further exploration of research behavior, rather than merely complex scaffolding". For the book that is a valuable corrective and belongs in the chapter's limitation paragraph and in ch17: harness engineering raises the ceiling on execution, and there is measured evidence that it does not by itself deliver research judgement. It also converges with Albayaydh et al. (theme A) on the finding that additional scaffolding does not consistently improve reliability. Caveats: unrefereed, and this sweep verified the abstract only; the benchmark is the authors' own construction and its claim to capture "research behaviour" is not independently validated; the named model and harness are products; and 68.3% on an author-defined benchmark is not directly comparable to anything. Cite the conclusion, not the number. For ai-writer: this belongs at the end of ch04 rather than the beginning, as the honest limit on everything the chapter has just argued. It also gives ch17 a measured counterweight to the capability-trend material already in `/research`, because it shows a frontier model in a well-built harness falling well short on the judgement-heavy parts of research work, which is exactly the boundary the book claims exists.

# What the sources say about claims the book currently makes

Read this section alongside `manuscript/RESTRUCTURE-PROPOSAL.md` §2, which lists what Ch. 2 displaces.

**1. Ch. 2 §2.1 attributes "harness" to practitioner commentary alone. This is now out of date.**
The draft reads: "one strand of practitioner commentary calls the whole assembly around the model the harness". As of this sweep the term is in use in the research literature, in paper titles and abstracts, by at least six independent groups (Guo et al. 2026; Zhou et al. 2026; Galster et al. 2026; Lin, M. et al. 2026; Lin, J. et al. 2026; Ben Sghaier et al. 2026; and Wang, J. et al. 2026 use "agentic harnesses" in a benchmark title). The sentence understates the term's standing and should be revised, with the citation moved from the video report to these sources. **This is the clearest correction this sweep produces.**

**2. Ch. 2 §2.4's middle-of-context claim is supported, and can now be cited.**
The claim that material at the start and end of a long context is attended to more reliably than material in the middle is supported by Liu et al. (TACL 2024), which is peer-reviewed. The existing "moderate confidence; the effect is documented but its magnitude varies by model" hedge is well calibrated and should stay.

**3. Ch. 2 §2.2's "silent wrong answer defeats the loop" is now strongly supported, from three directions.**
Babu and Agrawal (2026) show verifier-guided recovery driving silent failures to zero while non-verifying baselines return wrong-but-plausible outputs; Zhu et al. (2026) find recovery degrades most sharply under implicit semantic failures, attributed to over-trust in corrupted output; and TerraBench finds argument-value errors dominating failures in Earth-system tasks. The claim can be stated with high confidence and cited.

**4. Ch. 2 §2.6's "the dominant cost is verification" now has a cost the book has not accounted for.**
Bao et al. (2026) find that agentic pipelines increase inference energy substantially, particularly where the added steps are verification checks, without proportional quality gains. This does not contradict ch02 §2.6, which is about where effort concentrates, but it adds an environmental cost to the verification-first argument that an environmental-science book should not leave unstated. Recommend ch02 §2.6 or ch04 §4.8 acknowledges it.

**5. A caution the whole book should absorb: more harness is not reliably better.**
Three independent sources say so (Albayaydh et al. 2026, synthesis across 27 papers; Hajimiri et al. 2026, token-matched baselines; Wang, J. et al. 2026, research-behaviour benchmark), and two more report non-monotonic harness effects (Cho 2026; Lin, M. et al. 2026). Nothing in Ch. 2 contradicts this, but a harness chapter written without it would read as advocacy. **Recommend it be stated in §4.1 and again in the chapter's limitation paragraph.**

**6. No source located contradicts any Ch. 2 claim.** The failures found are of omission, not of error: Ch. 2 asserts several things it could not previously cite, and this sweep supplies citations for most of them.

# Where the literature is genuinely thin, quantified

These are findings, not apologies. Each tells the author which parts of Ch. 4 must be written from executed practice.

1. **No study measures whether an agent run record helps a human diagnose a run.** The observability literature (Dong 2024; Wang, Y. 2026; Ding 2026) proposes taxonomies and systems; none reports a user study, a time-to-diagnosis measurement, or any evidence that a conforming record is more useful than an ad-hoc one. **Zero sources located.** §4.6's practical guidance is therefore the book's own.
2. **No source gives an evidence-based step or token budget.** Sources establish that budgets are needed (Hou 2026), that budget-blind scaling plateaus (Liu 2025), and that fixed caps are the wrong instrument on their own (Shrivastava 2026). None recommends a number, and none could, since the right number is task-dependent. **Zero sources located** for a default.
3. **Wall-clock budgets for operational deadlines: nothing.** Latency appears in the cost literature as a system-performance metric, never as an operational constraint of the kind a forecast bulletin imposes. **Zero sources located.** This is the chapter's largest evidential hole and it happens to sit exactly where the author's own executed work is strongest.
4. **Backoff strategy for agents specifically: essentially nothing.** Retry *policy* is well covered; retry *timing* is not. One system paper mentions jittered exponential backoff in passing. Treat backoff as ordinary engineering practice needing no citation, rather than as a claim requiring one.
5. **Harness maintenance cadence: nothing.** Three sources establish that harnesses need maintenance and that model change is a trigger (Ben Sghaier 2026; Lin, M. 2026; Nate Jones in the video report). None studies how often, or what a review should check. **Zero sources located.**
6. **Environmental-science harness practice: two sources, neither about the harness.** TerraBench and Earth-Agent evaluate agents in the domain; AQUAH demonstrates one; none describes harness design decisions as such, and none reports logging, permissions, budgets or human gates for a scientific agent. **This is the sharpest gap in the sweep and the strongest argument for the book's positioning claim.** It also means every domain-specific harness statement in Ch. 4 must be `[AUTHOR: …]` or explicitly the book's judgement.
7. **Non-English and paywalled literature.** Not reached. See coverage limits.
