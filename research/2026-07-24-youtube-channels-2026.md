# @natebjones (Nate B Jones) — 6 videos reviewed, 6 selected

Nate B Jones is the strongest single source in this report for the book's purposes: his 2026 output consistently abstracts specific tool behaviour into named, reusable concepts (the "harness," the "agent run" as unit of analysis, "procedural debt") that map cleanly onto the book's own chapter structure, often independently converging on arguments the book already makes from the academic literature.

**"Don't build more AI agents until you watch this"**
Creator: Nate B Jones · Channel: @natebjones · Date: 17 June 2026
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BOXK2XFLA-E

The video's argument is that the difficult, durable problem in agent-building is not construction but maintenance of what Jones calls the "harness" — the sources an agent reads, the tools and permissions it holds, the proof it must produce, and the stop conditions that halt it — which must be actively maintained, including through deletion, as both the underlying model and the surrounding world change. Two evergreen concepts anchor the argument. First, agents fail in two directions ordinary software does not: "world drift" (a stale wiki, a changed metric definition, a moved process the agent ingests as ground truth and turns into confidently wrong output) and model improvement itself (restrictions and permissions calibrated for a weaker model become either pointlessly restrictive or, more dangerously, unsafe once a stronger model can take many plausible-looking actions in minutes, faster than a human can review) — a genuinely novel failure mode worth naming explicitly: software that breaks because the underlying model got better. Second, "pruning beats piling": the instinct when an agent underperforms is to add tools, rules and context, but the maintenance discipline is to ask what should be removed, illustrated by an anecdote (attributed to Vercel, second-hand and unverified independently) that an agent's performance improved after 80% of its tools were deleted. Jones also offers a five-point "agent health check" (source currency, permission reach, role drift, inspectable proof, ongoing value) mapping onto the book's verification-and-audit territory. For the book, this is strongest for ch02 (the harness concept), ch04 and ch13 (pruning, and the two-directions-of-failure framing as a named failure mode), and ch16 (harness ownership as a build-vs-adopt decision). Honesty note: based on a full transcript, judged complete and coherent with only minor caption artefacts; the Vercel anecdote and a "November to March" model-capability-jump claim are second-hand, unattributed to any primary source within the video, and need **[verify]**; the health-check framework is the presenter's own synthesis, not a citation to research, and should be presented as practitioner guidance, not established literature.

**"Opus 4.8 Scored 81. Your Workflow Doesn't Care."**
Creator: Nate B Jones · Channel: @natebjones · Date: 3 June 2026
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z73yuF14udI

The video argues that in 2026 the scaffolding around a model determines usefulness more than raw benchmark performance, so a nominally weaker model in a better harness beats a smarter model in a worse one, and organisations should architect for model swappability and outcome-based budgeting rather than commit to one vendor's frontier model. Three points are durable and tool-agnostic. First, "predictability as a first-class property": scaling up a model's reasoning effort no longer reliably improves results (the video cites the reviewed model scoring worse on a benchmark at a "high" reasoning setting than at a lower one) — an unpredictable model cannot be a dependable daily driver however capable on average, useful for ch04's adoption framing and ch11's evaluation chapter (variance matters as much as mean performance). Second, "harness co-evolution": scaffolding built to compensate for a weaker model's weaknesses becomes a drag once the model improves, so harnesses need scheduled reappraisal, not one-off design — relevant to ch02 and ch13. Third, the "piling problem": agents now generate work faster than humans can review it, so the fix is redesigning the whole pipeline to be "agent-native," with humans positioned "over the loop" (monitoring and designing it) rather than "in the loop" (approving every step) — useful for ch10's roster design and ch16's adoption planning. For the book: ch04, ch10, ch11 and ch13 are the strongest fits. Honesty note: based on a complete, coherent transcript; the video is heavily vendor- and product-specific (a model's version number and release date, adoption-share percentages attributed to an unspecified survey, an anecdotal "10 parallel threads" claim, a "dark factory" scenario) and none of these specifics should enter manuscript prose under the vendor-neutral rule; the benchmark regression and harness-co-evolution argument are the durable content, but the specific benchmark result and all named figures need **[verify]** before any quantitative use.

**"The Skill vs Prompt Problem Everyone Gets Wrong"**
Creator: Nate B Jones · Channel: @natebjones · Date: 19 June 2026
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9PUaEj0pMYE

This video argues that solving an agent's context or memory problem is insufficient, because even an agent that knows a user's background still has to be re-taught the user's procedures every session — "procedural debt" — and proposes that the fix is not a larger system prompt but a library of small, portable, inspectable "skills," each with defined triggers, boundaries, expected outputs and a verification method, composable into larger "runbooks." # @dylandavisai (Dylan Davis) — 5 videos reviewed, 5 selected

Dylan Davis's 2026 output is the most consistently rigorous, verification-focused material in this report after Nate Jones's: nearly every video reviewed proposes a specific, nameable procedure (a claim-audit workflow, a map-search-read pattern, a process-redesign heuristic, an elicitation method, a five-tactic trust framework) directly transferable to the book's ch03 and ch11 territory.

**"Claude and ChatGPT Hallucinate Less. That's Why They're Dangerous."**
Creator: Dylan Davis · Channel: @dylandavisai · Date: 9 May 2026
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n9rNzM-I82U

This video's central argument is that the most dangerous AI output is not the obviously wrong answer but the "almost right" one — fluent, mostly correct, with a small number of unsupported or misrepresented claims embedded in otherwise sound material — a failure mode that has become harder to catch precisely because overall hallucination rates have fallen, making confident, mostly-correct output the norm. Because simply asking a model "are you sure?" is unreliable, the presenter proposes a four-step claim-audit workflow for high-stakes outputs: finish the draft, decompose it into individually checkable atomic claims, verify each claim against source material with a four-way label (supported, conflicts with source, no evidence, needs human judgement), then rewrite from the audit — with each step run in a fresh conversation to prevent the model's own earlier reasoning from biasing its later self-check. This maps directly onto ch11 and offers a concrete, exportable procedure: proportionate verification (full audit reserved for genuinely high-stakes outputs, with an explicit "is this high-stakes?" checkpoint), claim decomposition, and context-fresh independent review are principles the book already argues from the academic literature (the first sweep's sycophancy and self-preference-bias sources), and this video is useful independent, practitioner-level corroboration, particularly the fresh-conversation-per-step independence principle relevant to ch07's reviewer-agent design. For the book: ch11 is the strongest fit, with secondary relevance to ch13 (the "almost right" failure mode) and ch04 (the high-stakes checkpoint). Honesty note: based on a complete, clean transcript; the worked example shown is explicitly illustrative rather than a real audited case, and the "90% of use cases don't need this" proportionality figure is presented as the presenter's own rule of thumb, not a study finding — it should be described as a practitioner heuristic, not measured evidence, and flagged **[verify]** if a number is wanted at all.

**"Claude Confidently Skipped Half Your Document and Didn't Tell You"**
Creator: Dylan Davis · Channel: @dylandavisai · Date: 16 May 2026
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ueNx7Wj9Rx4

This video's core argument is that context-window overflow produces a genuinely silent failure mode: browser-based chatbots given large or numerous files often read only part of the material yet still return a fluent, confident answer, with no warning — an explicit "file too big" error is, by comparison, the good outcome, because it is at least visible. For the book: ch13 and ch02 are the strongest fits, with secondary relevance to ch11. **"You're Automating Steps You Should Delete"**
Creator: Dylan Davis · Channel: @dylandavisai · Date: 20 July 2026
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vatOVZbumk0

This video's argument is that most organisations remain merely "AI-assisted" — bolting AI onto individual steps of an existing process and getting incrementally faster — while real transformation ("AI-native") comes from redesigning the process around AI's capabilities, typically by deleting steps that only existed to help humans coordinate with other humans. Its most durable concept is the "human tax": process steps that exist purely for human-to-human handoff, taking three forms — cleaning up messy material before passing it to a colleague (an AI can consume the messy input directly), producing a rough draft to economise a reviewer's effort (a full first version becomes cheap enough that reviewers can give better feedback on higher-fidelity output from the start), and reformatting between tools (which should be automated away). This gives ch04 a sharper version of its "augmentation versus automation" argument — not simply "should an agent do this step" but "should this step exist at all once an agent is available" — and directly supports ch10's discussion of where human gates belong: the presenter's own retained categories are exactly the work step that produces the result and the judgement or risk-bearing decision step, closely matching the book's human-gate philosophy. A second useful idea is working in token-efficient intermediate formats (plain text, markdown, CSV) throughout a pipeline and rendering a polished, human-facing artefact only at the end, relevant to ch02 and ch07. For the book: ch04 is the strongest fit, with secondary relevance to ch10 and ch02. Honesty note: based on a complete transcript; the specific figures offered (that roughly half the steps in a typical process can be cut; that some 95–99% of organisations remain merely AI-assisted) are explicitly the presenter's own consulting-practice estimates, not a cited study, and the seven-step-to-four-step worked example is illustrative rather than documented — both need **[verify]** or omission if precise figures are wanted in print.
